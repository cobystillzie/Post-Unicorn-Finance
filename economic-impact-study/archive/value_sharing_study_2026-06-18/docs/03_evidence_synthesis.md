## SCOPE & CORPUS. This synthesis grades the empirical evidence body of the NBER Shared Capitalism Research Project led by Douglas L. Kruse and Joseph R. Blasi (Rutgers School of Management and Labor Relations) and Richard B. Freeman (Harvard/NBER), plus two fully independent works that test the same hypotheses with stronger or pooled designs. The flagship output is the edited volume "Shared Capitalism at Work: Employee Ownership, Profit and Gain Sharing, and Broad-based Stock Options" (Kruse, Freeman & Blasi, eds., University of Chicago Press / NBER, April 2010). "Shared capitalism" = compensation tying worker pay or wealth to the performance of the firm/work group: employee ownership (notably ESOPs), individual stock ownership (ESPPs, 401(k) company stock), profit sharing, gain sharing, and broad-based stock options.  DATA (descriptive context, not a graded effect). Per the 2006 General Social Survey module, ~47% of US workers had at least one form of shared-capitalist pay (38% profit sharing, 27% gain sharing, 18% own company stock, 9% hold options), ~53.4 million workers (Introduction, c8085, p.13). The project built two datasets: (1) the NBER company survey — 41,206 employee responses across 14 firms / 323 worksites, 2001-2006, a SELF-SELECTED NON-RANDOM sample of firms (avg response rate 53%); and (2) shared-capitalism modules placed on the nationally representative GSS in 2002 (n=1,145) and 2006 (n=1,081), cross-sectional, not longitudinal.  IDENTIFICATION — THE CRUX FOR GRADING. The authors are unusually candid about causal limits and these self-disclosures, not external critics, drive my grades. From the Introduction (c8085): "Correlation between variables in nonexperimental survey research does not imply causation" (p.27); the firm sample is "self-selected nonrandom" (p.26); they tried instrumental-variables models but "could not identify any [instruments] that consistently met tests of exogeneity" (p.32); and "Without a genuine controlled experiment or some natural experiment that closely mimics a controlled experiment econometrics does not enable us to rule out the effect of unobserved factors on our results" (p.33). They describe their own approach as "quasi-experimental (Cook and Campbell 1979)" relying on within-firm variation with firm fixed effects and rich individual controls. Therefore the headline book claims ("shared capitalism IMPROVES firm performance / worker well-being") are the authors' framing of ASSOCIATIONS, and I grade the within-book survey findings as correlational, at best quasi-experimental — never causal-identified.  The book's causal support rests on three legs the authors themselves cite: (a) one within-project pre/post survey at a single firm before/after a profit-sharing plan (chapter 2); (b) a true field RCT by OTHER authors — Peterson & Luthans (2006), 3 of 21 fast-food stores randomly assigned profit sharing, where profit/productivity rose and turnover fell vs controls; and (c) a lab experiment, Frohlich et al. (1998), comparing employee-owned vs conventional "firms" of randomly assigned subjects. These are the genuinely causal-identified pieces, but each is tiny / single-firm / lab.  INDEPENDENT CORROBORATION. (1) Kim & Ouimet — a pre/post study of ESOPs adopted by US public companies 1980-2004 — found significant compensation increases after adoption, 4.5% long-term for ESOPs owning >5% of the company (quoted in chapter 11 / c8096, p.~, citing the 2008 Michigan working-paper draft; later published as "Broad-Based Employee Stock Ownership: Motives and Outcomes," Journal of Finance 69(3), 2014). Their broader result is conditional/mixed: small ESOPs (<5% of shares) at moderate-employee firms increase the pie for both workers and shareholders, but AVERAGE effects of large ESOPs are small because they are often adopted for non-incentive reasons (cash conservation, takeover defense). (2) O'Boyle, Patel & Gonzalez-Mulé, "Employee ownership and firm performance: a meta-analysis," Human Resource Management Journal 26(4), 2016 — 102 samples / 56,984 firms — found a SMALL, positive, statistically significant correlation, r = 0.04, robust across sampling designs (incl. pre/post adoption) and firm types, with non-US samples showing stronger effects. The convergence of these two independent works on a SMALL positive effect is the most balanced takeaway.  DIRECTION OF EFFECTS (balanced). Positive associations: turnover/quit intentions (lower), loyalty/organizational attachment (higher), willingness to work hard/effort (higher), co-worker effort and anti-shirking co-monitoring (higher), innovation culture, pay/benefits/wealth (higher), job security, satisfaction, labor-management relations. Crucially conditional: nearly all effects are largest only when shared capitalism is BUNDLED with employee involvement in decisions, low supervision, high-performance work practices, and at/above-market fixed wages; close supervision combined with shared capitalism has NEGATIVE effects on almost every outcome. Negative/null findings inside the body: absenteeism is HIGHER under shared capitalism (except when bundled with complementary practices) — the one consistently adverse outcome; in Dube & Freeman (chapter 5), shared-capitalist pay examined ALONE had "positive but generally statistically insignificant" effects on firm-benefiting behavior (the involvement committee did the work); and on inequality the effect is limited/mixed — shared capitalism "does little to reduce earnings inequality at [the lowest] income levels" and disproportionately excludes African-Americans and men with disabilities. On wealth/substitution, the book's regressions suggest employer stock complements rather than substitutes for pay (in 12 of 14 pay regressions the ownership coefficient was positive, significant-and-positive in 6), but this is correlational and from the same non-random sample.  BOTTOM LINE. The honest read: a large, internally consistent body of evidence shows shared capitalism is POSITIVELY ASSOCIATED with pro-firm worker behavior and worker well-being, the average effect is positive but MODEST and HETEROGENEOUS, it is strongest when combined with participatory practices, and it is NOT causally proven by the survey work. Independent, methodologically stronger work (Kim & Ouimet pre/post; O'Boyle et al. meta-analysis r=0.04) confirms a real but SMALL effect and adds conditionality (small ESOPs and competitive product markets channel gains to workers; large ESOPs often adopted for non-incentive reasons). The strongest causal evidence (Peterson & Luthans field RCT; Frohlich lab experiment) is directionally confirming but small-scale.

**Skeptical read:** SEPARATING INDEPENDENT RESEARCH FROM ADVOCACY. (1) Conflict-of-interest flag: Kruse and Blasi are not only the lead investigators but also direct/anchor the Rutgers Institute for the Study of Employee Ownership and Profit Sharing — i.e., researcher-advocates for the institutions they study, and the project received funding tied to the field. This warrants treating their headline framing ('shared capitalism improves firm performance / benefits workers') as advocacy-tinged. MITIGATING factors: the work is peer-reviewed (NBER/University of Chicago Press), the underlying GSS data are public, and the authors' OWN methodology section is unusually candid about causal limits (explicit 'correlation does not imply causation,' failed instruments, no controlled experiment). So I grade their work correlational/quasi-experimental — not advocacy-weak — because the data and caveats are transparent, while flagging the framing bias. (2) The genuinely independent tests converge on a SMALLER, more conditional effect than the book's prose implies: O'Boyle/Patel/Gonzalez-Mulé (2016) meta-analysis r=0.04 (tiny) and Kim & Ouimet (2014, JoF) showing large-ESOP average effects are small and often non-incentive-driven. This independent convergence is the most credible balanced conclusion. (3) Advocacy-weak material to discount: NCEO and employee-ownership-association talking points that cite the headline associations as if causal, and single-firm 'success stories' (Silicon Valley millionaire anecdotes) — directional/illustrative only. (4) Direction-of-causation remains open across the survey body: the positive associations could reflect shared capitalism CAUSING good outcomes, better firms/better workers SELECTING into shared capitalism, or both being driven by an unobserved 'corporate culture' latent variable — which the authors themselves name as the likely driver. (5) Generalizability: the 14-firm sample is self-selected and skews to large multinationals and committed ESOP firms; the GSS check helps external validity but is cross-sectional and thin in some cells. (6) Outcome-measurement: most NBER outcomes are employee-SELF-REPORTED attitudes/intentions (e.g., turnover intention, loyalty), not objective output or actual quits. (7) ANTI-HALLUCINATION DISCLOSURES: All numbers reported (47% prevalence, 53.4M, 41,206 respondents, 4.5% Kim-Ouimet, r=0.04, 102 samples/56,984 firms, 8% and 23% pay comparisons) were read from primary NBER PDF text I extracted locally (c8085, c8096) or, for the O'Boyle effect size, from a source-summary page after Wiley/SSRN returned HTTP 403. I did NOT independently re-read the published 2014 Journal of Finance or 2016 HRMJ abstracts (paywalled/403); their effect sizes are quoted as they appear in the NBER chapter text and the secondary summary, which I flag rather than present as personally verified from the journals of record. No figure here is estimated or invented.


## SYNTHESIS: Empirical evidence on ESOP firm performance, survival, and employee wealth — graded and source-separated.  COST NOTE TO USER: A session hook reports cost at $89.04 (flagged "critical"). I stopped further web research at that point and synthesized from sources already verified. A few NCEO wealth statistics below could not be traced to the primary study within budget; they are graded as advocacy/correlational and cited to NCEO (the source actually read), not to an unread primary.  === HOW TO READ THE GRADES === This literature is overwhelmingly CORRELATIONAL with documented self-selection: financially healthy, stable firms are disproportionately the ones that can afford to adopt an ESOP (a leveraged buyout requires creditworthiness and steady cash flow), so cross-sectional ESOP-vs-non-ESOP gaps conflate the effect of ownership with the pre-existing quality of adopters. NOTHING in the body I reviewed reaches "causal-identified" (no randomized field experiment on ESOPs exists — Kruse states this explicitly in IZA World of Labor). The strongest designs are QUASI-EXPERIMENTAL (matched-sample survival models; pre/post panels; Census-linked employer-employee data). I grade on TWO axes: (1) method quality (causal-identified / quasi-experimental / correlational / advocacy-weak) and (2) independent-vs-advocacy provenance, kept separate — an NCEO study on administrative data can be methodologically reasonable yet advocacy-affiliated and non-peer-reviewed.  === INDEPENDENT PEER-REVIEWED RESEARCH (the credible core) ===  1) META-ANALYSIS OF FIRM PERFORMANCE — O'Boyle, Patel & Gonzalez-Mulé (2016), "Employee ownership and firm performance: a meta-analysis," Human Resource Management Journal 26(4):424-448. Pooled 102 samples / 56,984 firms. Result: small, positive, statistically significant association, mean r̄ = 0.04. Positive across sampling designs, performance measures (efficiency and growth), and firm types (public/private); US effect positive but SMALLER than international. GRADE: correlational (it aggregates mostly correlational primaries; meta-analysis does not manufacture causal identification). This is the single best summary statistic of the field and the honest headline: the effect is real but small and not identified as causal. Verified via Wiley landing page + indexing.  2) PUBLIC-FIRM SURVIVAL — Park, Kruse & Sesil (2004), "Does Employee Ownership Enhance Firm Survival?", Advances in the Economic Analysis of Participatory and Labor-Managed Firms, Vol. 8 (Emerald/JAI). All US public companies 1988→2001, Weibull survival models vs a CLOSELY MATCHED comparison sample. Firms with employee-ownership stakes ≥5% were only ~76% as likely to disappear (≈ "20% more likely to survive over 12 years," per Kruse's IZA restatement). Notably, the authors report the survival edge is NOT explained by higher productivity, financial strength, or compensation flexibility — they attribute it to greater employment stability. GRADE: quasi-experimental (matched sample + duration model), still NOT causal. Verified via CLEO/Rutgers landing page.  3) PRIVATE-FIRM SURVIVAL — Blasi, Kruse & Weltmann (2013), "Firm Survival and Performance in Privately Held ESOP Companies," Advances in the Economic Analysis of Participatory and Labor-Managed Firms, Vol. 14. Dun & Bradstreet population of privately held ESOP firms, 1988-1999, matched to same-industry non-ESOP firms. Private ESOPs were about HALF as likely to go bankrupt or close, and roughly three-fifths (≈60%) as likely to disappear for any reason. GRADE: quasi-experimental (matched panel), correlational on causation. Verified via Emerald/Rutgers indexing + NCEO summary.  4) WAGES / EMPLOYMENT — Kim & Ouimet (2014), "Broad-Based Employee Stock Ownership: Motives and Outcomes," The Journal of Finance 69(3):1273-1319. US Census employer-employee data. NUANCED, partly DEFLATIONARY finding: SMALL ESOPs (<5% of shares) "increase the economic pie," benefiting both employees and shareholders; but LARGE ESOPs are often adopted for NON-incentive reasons (conserving cash by substituting shares for wages; forming worker-management alliances to deter takeovers), so AVERAGE wage/productivity impacts are SMALL. Employee wages (excluding ESOP contributions) rose for small ESOPs and were roughly stable for large ESOPs. GRADE: quasi-experimental (Census panel, attention to adoption motives = the closest thing to selection-aware identification here). This is the best independent corrective to advocacy framing: ownership is not uniformly value-creating; motive matters.  5) THE PARTICIPATION CONTINGENCY (skeptical, independent) — US GAO (late 1980s) concluded employee ownership does NOT by itself raise productivity or profitability ABSENT worker participation in decisions; early studies (e.g., participative vs non-participative ESOPs) show gains concentrate in participatory firms (non-participative ESOPs showed no improvement). GRADE: correlational/mixed, but a credible independent counterweight: "ESOP" is not a monolith; the structural detail (participation) drives results. Cited via NCEO's own summary of GAO.  === ADVOCACY / AFFILIATED RESEARCH (use with the provenance caveat) ===  6) RETIREMENT WEALTH — NCEO syntheses. Reported figures: median ESOP account balance ~$80,500 vs ~$30,000 for comparable non-ESOP workers (2023); ESOP participants hold ~2.2x as much in retirement plans and ~20% more total financial assets (2010, Form 5500-based); being in an ESOP associated with 92% higher median household net wealth, 33% higher wages, 53% longer tenure for ages 28-34 (2017, Kellogg-funded). GRADE: correlational + advocacy-affiliated. The underlying administrative data (DOL Form 5500, General Social Survey) can be sound, but these are NCEO-produced, largely non-peer-reviewed, cross-sectional, and not selection-corrected — workers at ESOP firms differ systematically (more stable firms, longer tenure by construction). Direction (positive on wealth) is consistent and plausibly partly real, but magnitudes should not be read as causal. Cited to NCEO (the source read).  7) RETENTION / PANDEMIC RESILIENCE — NCEO + Rutgers/Employee Ownership Foundation (2020-2023). Reported: voluntary quit rates ~1/3 of national average (2023); ESOP firms 3-4x more likely to retain staff during COVID, far fewer pay cuts (26.9% vs 57.3%); GSS 2002-2022: employee-owners laid off 1.9% vs 5.1%. GRADE: mixed — the GSS layoff differential survives controls for tenure/occupation/demographics (quasi-experimental-leaning), but the survey/cross-section design and advocacy funding warrant caution. Direction (positive on retention/stability) is the most consistently replicated result across independent and advocacy work alike.  === THE SELECTION-BIAS DEBATE (verbatim from the lead independent researcher) === Kruse, "Does employee ownership improve performance?", IZA World of Labor (verified full text): "Of course, correlation does not imply causation." On survival specifically: "Causation is an open question in the company stability and survival results, since it may be that more stable companies are more likely to adopt employee ownership." And: "there have not been true field experiments to clearly demonstrate a causal effect of employee ownership." This is the honest center of gravity: even the field's most prominent advocates-as-researchers concede the causal question is open and that reverse causation (healthy firm → ESOP, not ESOP → healthy firm) is unrefuted.  === BOTTOM LINE === Robust and replicated: a SMALL positive performance association (r≈0.04), and a MODERATE, well-identified survival/stability advantage (matched-sample designs, public and private). Genuinely contested/weak: causal attribution (none), and the large wealth multipliers from advocacy sources (plausible direction, inflated/uncorrected magnitudes). The most credible independent nuance (Kim & Ouimet) is that benefits depend on WHY the ESOP was adopted, and GAO/participation work shows benefits depend on whether workers actually participate — both cut against a simple "ESOPs are good for firms" narrative.

**Caveats on the above:**
- Meta-analysis of predominantly cross-sectional, correlational primaries; aggregation does not establish causation. Effect size is small (r=0.04). Verified via Wiley landing page + ResearchGate indexing, not the full PDF.
- Matched-sample Weibull survival model strengthens inference but is not causal identification. Authors state the survival edge is NOT explained by productivity, financial strength, or pay flexibility, attributing it to employment stability. Reverse causation (stable firms adopt EO) remains possible.
- Dun & Bradstreet population matched to non-ESOP firms; strong design but correlational on causation. Numbers confirmed via Rutgers/Emerald indexing and NCEO summary; full PDF not parsed within budget.
- Strongest independent identification (US Census employer-employee data, attention to adoption motive). Deflates the uniform 'ESOPs raise pay/productivity' claim: effects depend on size/motive and average impacts are small. Abstract verified via Wiley/SSRN; full text not parsed.

**Skeptical read:** SELECTION BIAS IS THE CENTRAL UNRESOLVED PROBLEM. Adopting an ESOP — especially a leveraged buyout — requires a creditworthy, cash-flow-stable firm. The field's own leading researcher concedes this in print (Kruse, IZA World of Labor): "correlation does not imply causation," "there have not been true field experiments to clearly demonstrate a causal effect," and on survival specifically "it may be that more stable companies are more likely to adopt employee ownership." NO study in this body is causal-identified; the best are quasi-experimental (matched samples, Census panels).  INDEPENDENT vs ADVOCACY MUST BE KEPT SEPARATE. ## SCOPE AND METHOD: This grades the empirical evidence on worker-cooperative survival and productivity relative to conventional firms, drawing on the most-cited independent studies (Burdín; Fakhfakh, Pérotin & Gago; Pencavel et al.; the Olsen/CECOP/Pérotin survival literature) plus descriptive Mondragon case evidence. Provenance caution: two primary-text fetches failed (the EFES PDF returned as binary; the SAGE Burdín 2014 page returned HTTP 403). The Burdín headline number was therefore verified against the open-access working-paper version (Burdín 2012, IECON/Universidad de la República, repec ulr/wpaper dt-06-12), which states a 24-38% lower exit hazard; the published ILR Review 2014 version reports 29% as the central estimate after excluding microenterprises and controlling for the effective tax burden. Other numbers (French SCOP rates, Mondragon counts, Pencavel Italian figures) come from search-surfaced summaries of named studies, not from primary text I read end-to-end; they are reported with that caveat and should be checked against the primary PDF before paper use.  CEILING ON RIGOR: No study here is a randomized or cleanly causal-identified design. The strongest work (Burdín's frailty duration models; the matched-sample French survival analyses; the Fakhfakh-Pérotin-Gago production-function estimation) is quasi-experimental at best -- observational panels with controls and matching, vulnerable to selection (who chooses the co-op form, which firms convert, survivorship in the registry). Treat "quasi-experimental" as the top grade awarded and read every positive finding as association under controls, not proof of a causal co-op effect.  CRITICAL DE-DUPLICATION: A widely circulated claim that "cooperatives survive 29% longer (Pérotin)" is NOT an independent finding. It is Burdín's Uruguay hazard result ("29% lower hazard... accounting for firm size, capital, and industry") re-cited inside Pérotin's review and frequently re-attributed to her by secondary summaries. It is listed ONCE below, under Burdín. Pérotin's contribution is a synthesis/review, not a separate survival dataset.  INDEPENDENT RESEARCH vs ADVOCACY: The academic productivity finding is narrow -- cooperatives are "at least as productive" as conventional firms (Fakhfakh, Pérotin & Gago 2012, ILR Review). The stronger popular claim -- co-ops are "more productive," staff "work better and smarter" -- comes from Pérotin's general-audience writing and co-op-movement outlets (Co-operatives UK, Shareable, Democracy at Work) and is graded advocacy-weak. The rigorous study should not lend its credibility to the stronger gloss. Similarly, Mondragon's 97% survival statistic is descriptive case-study material from co-op-aligned and case sources and must be balanced against the 2013 collapse of Fagor Electrodomésticos, Mondragon's flagship appliance cooperative.  NET READ: The often-asserted theoretical prediction that worker co-ops are competitively fragile is not supported by the better observational evidence -- in Uruguay and (raw) in France, co-ops survive at least as well as or better than conventional firms, plausibly via wage-flexibility/employment-stability mechanisms (cut pay before cutting jobs). But the survival edge is partly or wholly explained by firm characteristics at creation once samples are matched (French evidence), and productivity is "comparable," not dramatically superior. The honest summary is: co-ops are a viable, not a fragile, organizational form, with comparable productivity and somewhat greater employment stability -- a defensible but not spectacular empirical case, and one where advocacy framing routinely overstates effect sizes and overclaims causality.

- Italian panel evidence that co-ops adjust pay more and employment less than capitalist firms; observational. 'Mechanism' linkage to survival is inferential, not directly tested in a single causal design. Numbers below for the wage/capital findings come from the same study.
- This is a WAGE/CAPITAL finding, not a productivity or survival finding -- direction is genuinely mixed: lower and more variable pay is a worker cost, but it is the flip side of the greater employment stability above (pay flexes so jobs persist). Often mis-cited as evidence co-ops 'underperform'; that reading is unsupported. Figures from secondary summaries of the study, not primary text read here.
- Single-group descriptive case, no matched conventional comparison; survivorship and reporting bias likely. Must be balanced against the 2013 bankruptcy of Fagor Electrodomésticos, Mondragon's flagship appliance co-op (~5,600 jobs), which shows the model is not failure-proof. Treat the 97% figure as illustrative case evidence, not a generalizable rate.

**Skeptical read:** PROVENANCE IS THE MAIN RISK ON THIS TASK. Only the Burdín working paper and the identification of the Fakhfakh-Pérotin-Gago and Pencairo-Pistaferri-Schivardi citations were verified against primary or near-primary text. The EFES/Pérotin PDF returned unreadable binary and the SAGE Burdín 2014 page returned 403, so the French SCOP rates, the Mondragon counts, and the Pencavel Italian numbers rest on web-search summaries of named, real studies -- not on text I read end-to-end. Every such number is flagged in its caveat. Before any of these figures is used in a published paper, fetch the primary PDF and confirm the exact value.  CONFLATION CAUGHT AND CORRECTED: The popular 'co-ops survive 29% longer (Pérotin)' claim is Burdín's Uruguay hazard result re-cited inside Pérotin's review; it is listed once under Burdín, not as a second independent finding. Emitting it twice would have been fabrication-by-conflation.  SELECTION IS THE DEEPEST THREAT TO VALIDITY. Co-ops are not randomly assigned. The firms that adopt the co-op form, or that get converted via worker buyout, differ systematically from conventional firms (sector, founder motivation, pre-existing health). The French matched-sample result -- survival distributions converge once you match on firm characteristics -- is the single most important skeptical fact in this whole body: it implies the raw survival advantage is substantially a composition effect. Burdín's frailty models partly address unobserved heterogeneity but cannot fully rule out selection on the choice of organizational form.  PUBLICATION AND ADVOCACY TILT. A large share of accessible secondary material comes from co-op-movement bodies (Co-operatives UK, CECOP/CICOPA, Democracy at Work, Shareable). These reliably report the favorable studies and the favorable framings ('more productive', 'better and smarter') that exceed what the peer-reviewed work actually shows ('at least as productive'). The independent academic core (Burdín; Fakhfakh-Pérotin-Gago; Pencavel et al.) is more measured than its popularization.  WHAT THE EVIDENCE DOES NOT SHOW. There is no causal-identified (RCT or natural-experiment-with-clean-identification) evidence that converting a firm to worker ownership raises its survival or productivity. The defensible conclusions are bounded: (1) co-ops are NOT more failure-prone than conventional firms and are plausibly somewhat more durable, largely via wage-flexible employment stability; (2) productivity is comparable, not clearly superior; (3) Mondragon is an impressive but non-generalizable case with at least one major failure (Fagor). Claims beyond these -- especially large productivity premia or guaranteed resilience -- outrun the evidence.


## SKEPTICAL / CRITICAL EVIDENCE ON EMPLOYEE OWNERSHIP — SYNTHESIS AND GRADING  Scope and honest framing: This body assembles the *critical* case against employee ownership (EO) — free-rider/1-N theory, selection bias/endogeneity, mixed/null results, underperformance cases, and worker risk-concentration. The most defensible net reading is NOT "EO fails." It is threefold: (1) the headline positive correlations are SMALLER than advocacy claims and largely NOT causally identified (selection/endogeneity is the strongest skeptical point); (2) several classic skeptical PREDICTIONS (free-rider shirking, the cooperative horizon/underinvestment problem) are theory that the empirical record only PARTLY confirms or even refutes; and (3) the cleanest empirically-grounded NEGATIVE is risk-concentration/under-diversification, because it follows from portfolio theory independent of whether the firm performs well. The forms must not be blurred: free-rider applies most to broad profit-sharing/group-incentive and equity; risk-concentration to ESOPs and company stock in 401(k)s; the horizon problem to worker cooperatives / labor-managed firms.  INDEPENDENT vs ADVOCACY: Independent/peer-reviewed sources used here include the O'Boyle/Patel/Gonzalez-Mulé meta-analysis (Human Resource Management Journal, 2016), Doucouliagos (Industrial and Labor Relations Review, 1995), Meulbroek (Harvard Business School working paper / Financial Management), Benartzi et al. (University of Chicago Law Review), Furubotn & Pejovich (Zeitschrift für Nationalökonomie, 1970), and the United case analysis in California Management Review (2018). The NBER "Shared Capitalism at Work" volume (Kruse/Blasi/Freeman) is EO-sympathetic and Rutgers-affiliated but is rigorous NBER/University of Chicago Press work that itself models and tests free-riding honestly — graded on method, not author. Genuinely advocacy-aligned bodies (NCEO, Rutgers Institute, ESOP Association, Project Equity) are NOT used as the basis for any numeric claim here.  KEY FINDINGS:  1. SMALL EFFECT SIZE (meta-analysis). O'Boyle, Patel & Gonzalez-Mulé (2016, HRM Journal) pooled 102 samples / 56,984 firms and found EO has a positive but very small association with firm performance, r = 0.04. The skeptical reading: the effect is real but modest and far smaller than advocacy rhetoric implies; effects vary by design and firm type. This is the single best summary statistic against over-strong EO claims. Grade: correlational meta-analysis (not causally identified — most input studies are cross-sectional).  2. SELECTION BIAS / ENDOGENEITY (the strongest skeptical point, conceded by proponents). Across the EO literature, ownership structure is not randomly assigned: higher-performing, more stable firms are more likely to adopt EO, and longer-tenure workers self-select into firms offering it. This means cross-sectional positive correlations cannot be read as EO *causing* performance/survival — reverse causality is plausible. O'Boyle et al. (2016) explicitly distinguish pre-post (change) designs from cross-sectional ones precisely because the latter cannot rule this out. Direction is "mixed" rather than negative: once selection is addressed (pre-post, DID, IV, propensity matching), effects typically shrink but do not vanish. Grade: this is a methodological critique that DOWNGRADES the positive literature to correlational.  3. FREE-RIDER / 1-N PROBLEM (theory only partly confirmed). Standard incentive theory predicts that in group-based reward schemes (profit-sharing, broad equity), each worker captures only ~1/N of the value of their own extra effort, so the rational response is to shirk and free-ride. The NBER Shared Capitalism research (Freeman, Kruse & Blasi, "Worker Responses to Shirking under Shared Capitalism," in *Shared Capitalism at Work*, 2010; data from 2002/2006 GSS + ~40,000 employees at 14 firms) confirms the *prediction* but shows it is PARTLY OVERCOME: workers with a larger financial stake are more likely to monitor co-workers and intervene against shirking (peer/co-monitoring and anti-shirking norms). So the honest direction is "mixed": free-riding is a genuine drag that is mitigated, not a confirmed failure — and the mitigation appears tied to participatory management. Grade: quasi-experimental/survey evidence; the 1-N effect itself is a theoretical prediction.  4. WORKER RISK-CONCENTRATION / UNDER-DIVERSIFICATION (the cleanest empirical negative; financial economics). Meulbroek (2002, HBS working paper 02-058; published in Financial Management) shows that because company stock is undiversified and its value is correlated with the worker's own human capital/job, an employee holding it sacrifices a large share of its market value as un-compensated risk: on her assumptions an employee values $1 of company stock at far below $1, with the discount widening with horizon and allocation (e.g., ~58 cents on the dollar at a 10-year horizon / 25% allocation, falling to ~33 cents at 15 years / 50% allocation; an average ~42% of market value forgone). This critique does not depend on firm performance — it is portfolio theory. Direction: negative (a real welfare cost to worker-owners). Grade: theoretical/quantitative modeling (strong internal logic; not an empirical estimate of realized losses).  5. COMPANY STOCK IN 401(k) PLANS — MISPERCEIVED, CORRELATED RISK (independent law-and-economics). Benartzi, Thaler, Utkus & Sunstein, "The Law and Economics of Company Stock in 401(k) Plans" (University of Chicago Law Review / Journal of Law and Economics, ~2007) documents that millions of participants over-invest in employer stock and that workers systematically MISPERCEIVE the risk — only ~18% recognized employer stock as riskier than a diversified stock fund. Because the asset's value correlates with the worker's earnings, a downturn threatens job AND retirement savings simultaneously. The canonical underperformance illustration: at Enron's 2002 collapse, ~62% of its 401(k) assets were in Enron stock and employees lost jobs and retirement savings together. Direction: negative (wealth/risk). Grade: independent empirical + case evidence for the misperception; the Enron figure is a single dramatic case (case-study weight, not generalizable on its own).  6. COOPERATIVE HORIZON / UNDERINVESTMENT PROBLEM (classic theory — largely REFUTED empirically). Furubotn & Pejovich (1970, "Property Rights and the Behavior of the Firm in a Socialist State: The Example of Yugoslavia," Zeitschrift für Nationalökonomie 30:431-454) argued worker-members of labor-managed firms with non-transferable, non-recoupable equity claims have a truncated time horizon and will therefore UNDER-invest and undercapitalize ("self-extinction"/horizon problem). This is a foundational skeptical prediction specific to worker cooperatives. Direction: "mixed"/refuted — the empirical literature finds the hypothesized underinvestment generally does NOT occur in practice (cooperatives such as Mondragón solve it via individual internal capital accounts, and observed co-ops often plough back MORE profit than required, plausibly as job-loss insurance). Grade: theoretical (the original is a property-rights model; subsequent evidence weighs against it).  7. UNDERPERFORMANCE CASE — UNITED AIRLINES ESOP (n=1, do not over-weight). In 1994 United (UAL) employees acquired ~53-55% of the company (the largest U.S. majority-employee-owned firm at the time) in exchange for ~$4.8-5 billion in concessions including ~15% wage cuts; UAL filed Chapter 11 in December 2002 and the ESOP shares became nearly worthless. Ossi, "United We Fall: An Analysis of ESOP Work Culture" (California Management Review, 2018) attributes the failure to financial ownership WITHOUT genuine governance participation (an "incentive program," not ownership culture), exclusion of flight attendants, and defensive/adversarial use of the ESOP — on-time performance collapsed from 81% to ~40% amid a 2000 pilot slowdown. The skeptical lesson is conditional: equity alone, absent participatory management, does not deliver — consistent with finding #3. Direction: negative for this case. Grade: advocacy-weak/single case study; 9/11 and sector-wide airline distress are major confounds — do not let this anecdote outweigh the meta-analyses.  OVERALL: The critical literature is best characterized as "effects are smaller and less causally identified than advocates claim; free-rider and horizon predictions are only partly borne out (and the horizon problem is largely refuted); the firmest negative is concentrated, under-diversified, often-misperceived financial risk to worker-owners." Almost none of these findings — positive OR negative — rest on clean causal identification; that non-identification is itself a central skeptical meta-finding.

- This is a methodological critique, not a +/- finding: encoded as mixed because once selection is controlled (pre-post, DID, IV, propensity matching) effects typically shrink but do not vanish. The strongest single skeptical point; it downgrades the entire positive literature to correlational. Reverse causality (good firms adopt EO) is the core risk.
- Form: broad profit-sharing/group-incentive and shared equity. The 1-N effect is a theoretical prediction; data (2002/2006 GSS + ~40,000 employees at 14 firms) show free-riding is mitigated, not eliminated, and mitigation is linked to participatory management. Authors are EO-sympathetic (Rutgers-affiliated) but the work models free-riding honestly. NOT a confirmed EO failure.
- Graded conservatively: it is rigorous financial-economics modeling, NOT empirical realized-loss data, so the precise 42%/58c/33c figures are model outputs (binary PDF not directly parsed; figures from the HBS-hosted source and SSRN abstract). This is the cleanest skeptical strand because it follows from portfolio theory regardless of firm performance. Form: ESOPs / company stock.
- Figures (18% risk-perception; Enron 62%) from the UCLA Anderson-hosted manuscript as surfaced in search (binary PDF not directly parsed). Independent law-and-economics, but the Enron 62% is a single dramatic case (case-study weight). Form: company stock in defined-contribution plans, distinct from controlled ESOPs.
- Form: worker cooperatives / labor-managed firms (a distinct skeptical strand from ESOPs). The 1970 paper is a theoretical model; graded advocacy-weak as a *skeptical empirical* claim because subsequent evidence weighs AGAINST the underinvestment prediction. Direction mixed/refuted, not negative. Included for balance: a classic critique that the data did not bear out.
- Single case (n=1). The CMR article gives the 81%->40% on-time figure and the culture/governance argument but NOT the ownership %/wage numbers; the ~53-55% ownership and ~15%/$4.8-5bn concession figures are sourced separately (Rutgers CLEO history). Major confounds: 9/11 and sector-wide airline distress. Do not weight above the meta-analyses; the lesson is conditional (equity without participation underdelivers).

**Skeptical read:** EVIDENCE-QUALITY HIERARCHY (most to least probative as SKEPTICAL evidence): (1) Selection/endogeneity is the strongest and most general critique — it is conceded even by proponents and downgrades the bulk of the positive EO literature to correlational; almost no finding (positive or negative) is causally identified, which is itself the central meta-finding. (2) Risk-concentration / under-diversification (Meulbroek; Benartzi et al.) is the cleanest empirically-grounded NEGATIVE because it derives from portfolio theory independent of firm performance and is corroborated by worker risk-misperception data and the Enron case. (3) The small meta-analytic effect (O'Boyle r=0.04) is the best skeptical summary statistic — real but modest, undercutting strong advocacy claims. (4) Free-rider/1-N and the cooperative horizon problem are PREDICTIONS the evidence only partly confirms or actively refutes — they belong in a balanced skeptical body but must be flagged as theory, not confirmed failures (hence direction 'mixed', not 'negative'). (5) Underperformance anecdotes (United, Enron) are n=1 cases with heavy confounds (9/11, fraud) and the lowest probative weight; they illustrate, they do not generalize.  BALANCE / FABRICATION DISCIPLINE: The defensible synthesis is 'effects are smaller and less causally identified than advocates claim, and worker-owners bear concentrated undiversified risk' — NOT 'employee ownership fails.' Three of the seven findings are skeptical predictions the data partially or wholly refute, and are labeled accordingly to avoid overstating the critical case.
