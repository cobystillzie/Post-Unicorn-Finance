# Mechanism Catalog — Worker Value-Sharing

Broad-and-deep catalog of mechanisms for sharing a company's created value with its workers. Every entry is source-cited from the Phase-0 research workflow (2026-06-17); each was generated under an anti-hallucination rule (real source URL required or the value is omitted). **46 mechanisms** across 37 categories.

> Posture: descriptive. Inclusion here is *not* an endorsement or a claim of impact — the causal question is handled separately in `03_evidence_synthesis.md`.


## Quick index — mechanisms a generalist VC likely does NOT know

Extracted for the conversation with Ethan (his "possibly even terms I do not know").

- **Apis & Heritage Capital Partners — Employee-Led Buyout (ELBO)** — A mission-driven fund that finances the purchase of profitable companies from retiring owners and converts them to 100% employee ownership, explicitly to close the racial wealth gap.
- **Broad-based equity / carry sharing with portfolio-company employees (Blackstone, KKR)** — Large buyout firms granting equity (or value tied to the firm's investment return) to ALL employees of an acquired portfolio company, not just senior management.
- **Doppelstiftung (double-foundation model)** — A German ownership structure that splits a company's economic ownership from its voting control: a charitable foundation holds the large majority of the share capital (dividends fund charity), while a separate trust/entity holds the majority of the voting rights to steer the company long-term.
- **Employee Ownership Trust (EOT)** — A UK trust that holds a permanent or long-term controlling shareholding in a company on behalf of ALL the company's employees collectively, rather than allocating shares to employees individually. Modeled conceptually on the John Lewis Partnership (which predates the statute) and given a statutory tax definition by the Finance Act 2014.
- **Employee Stock Ownership Plan (ESOP)** — A tax-qualified retirement plan, held in a trust, that is designed to invest primarily in the stock of the sponsoring employer, giving employees an ownership stake in the company they work for, typically at no out-of-pocket cost.
- **Employee-Ownership Conversion** — The PROCESS of transitioning a business from conventional (founder/family/investor) ownership to employee ownership - selling or transferring the business to its workers - executed through one of several end-state vehicles: an ESOP, a worker cooperative, or an employee ownership trust.
- **Enterprise / Industrial Foundation (Danish Model)** — A foundation that owns and controls an operating company. The foundation is a self-governing, ownerless legal entity with no shareholders to whom it pays dividends; it holds a controlling block of the company (often a minority of share capital but a majority of voting rights) and is actively involved in the business while channeling surplus to research and charitable purposes.
- **Equity crowdfunding participation by employees (Regulation Crowdfunding)** — Using the securities-crowdfunding exemption to let a broad base of small investors — potentially including a company's own employees — buy equity in the company.
- **Exit to Community (E2C)** — An alternative startup 'exit' in which a company transitions from investor ownership to ownership by its community of stakeholders — employees, users, or customers — instead of an IPO or acquisition.
- **Golden Share / Veto Share Model** — A single special class of share that carries little or no economic value but holds defined VETO rights, used (in the steward-ownership context) to give an independent mission-guardian - typically a foundation - the legal power to block changes that would betray a company's purpose, especially attempts to sell control or to extract profits for private gain.
- **Henkilostorahasto (Finnish Personnel Fund)** — A Finnish fund owned and controlled by a company's or agency's own personnel that collectively receives the employer's performance/profit bonuses and allocates them to members as personal fund units, functioning as a collective reward and savings mechanism.
- **Interessement (voluntary profit/performance-sharing, France)** — A French optional value-sharing scheme letting any company tie employee bonuses to the company's results or performance, under a collective agreement, with tax/social advantages - separate from and additional to mandatory participation.
- **Lontagarfonderna (Swedish wage-earner funds) - HISTORICAL/ABOLISHED** — A 1980s Swedish system of collective, union-influenced funds intended to gradually shift corporate ownership toward employees/society by channeling a slice of company profits into regional funds that bought shares - a far more limited version of the original radical 'Meidner plan'.
- **Marcora Law worker buyout (workers' buyout / WBO)** — An Italian legal framework that lets employees of a company facing closure pool their would-be unemployment benefits as a lump sum to capitalize a new worker cooperative that buys out the failing firm, backed by matching public/cooperative finance.
- **Mosaic Capital Partners — ESOP-focused private equity** — A private-equity fund dedicated to acquiring lower-middle-market companies and structuring each as an ESOP buyout so employees ultimately own 100% of the company.
- **Nonqualified Deferred Compensation (NQDC) — top-hat plans, SERPs, deferred comp** — A legally binding right to compensation that is earned in one year but paid (and taxed) in a later year, offered to a select group of management or highly compensated employees outside the qualified-plan limits.
- **Ownership Works (shared-ownership nonprofit; KKR/Pete Stavros model)** — A nonprofit that helps private-equity firms and companies give ALL employees (not just executives) an equity stake in the companies they work for, so workers share in value creation at exit.
- **Participation (mandatory profit-sharing, France)** — A French legal mandate requiring companies above a size threshold to redistribute a portion of their profits to all employees via a formula fixed by law, building employee savings rather than tying payouts to individual performance.
- **Perpetual Purpose Trust (PPT)** — A non-charitable trust created to advance a defined PURPOSE (a mission) rather than to benefit named individuals, and which may continue indefinitely. When it owns a company's voting shares, the trust effectively takes the seat of a shareholder; because a trust is not a person, profits cannot be extracted for any individual's personal gain.
- **Phantom Stock** — A contractual promise to pay a future cash (or stock) bonus equal to the value of a set number of notional company shares — or the increase in that value — without issuing any real equity, voting rights, or ownership.
- **Profit-Sharing Plan (including 401(k) profit-sharing)** — A qualified retirement plan into which the employer makes discretionary contributions out of company profits to employees' retirement accounts; when bolted onto a 401(k), employees can also defer their own wages.
- **Profits Interest (LLC) / Management Incentive Units** — A grant of an equity stake in an LLC or partnership that entitles the holder only to a share of FUTURE profits and appreciation above a threshold value set at grant — with no claim on the entity's existing capital — used as the equity-incentive vehicle for businesses taxed as partnerships.
- **Profits interest (LLC equity grant / carry-style worker equity)** — An LLC/partnership equity grant giving an employee a share of the company's FUTURE profits and appreciation above a grant-date threshold, with no claim on existing value.
- **Purpose Foundation / golden-share steward-ownership enabler** — A non-profit intermediary that helps companies lock in steward-ownership by taking a tiny 'golden' veto share, ensuring the company's purpose and the no-sale/no-profit-extraction principles can never be reversed by future owners.
- **Sociedad Laboral (SAL / SLL) - worker-owned company** — A Spanish commercial company (a public limited or limited-liability company) in which the majority of the share capital is owned by employees who have permanent, full-time employment contracts - legally distinct from a worker cooperative.
- **Societe cooperative et participative (SCOP)** — A French worker/production cooperative in which employees are the majority owner-members, holding at least the majority of capital and the majority of voting rights on a one-person-one-vote basis, with profits shared substantially with the workforce.
- **Steward-Ownership (Verantwortungseigentum)** — An ownership model (German term Verantwortungseigentum, 'responsible/responsibility ownership') built on two binding principles: (1) self-determination/self-governance - voting control stays with people connected to the company's mission and the company cannot become an object of speculation; and (2) purpose-orientation - profits are a means to an end, are reinvested or used for the mission/common good, and cannot be extracted for owners' personal enrichment ('asset lock').
- **Stichting Administratiekantoor (STAK) with depositary receipts** — A Dutch trust-office foundation that holds the legal title and voting rights to a company's shares and issues 'depositary receipts' (certificaten) to beneficiaries, splitting voting control (kept by the foundation's board/stewards) from the economic benefit (dividends, flowing to receipt-holders).
- **Stock Appreciation Right (SAR)** — A right to receive the monetary equivalent of the increase in the value of a specified number of company shares over a set period, with no upfront purchase required; payable in cash, in shares, or a combination.
- **Teamshares (employee-ownership buyout platform)** — A venture-backed company that buys small businesses from retiring owners and gradually converts them into majority employee-owned firms, granting workers stock at no cost to them.
- **Tokenized employee ownership (equity/token grants on-chain)** — Representing employee ownership stakes as blockchain tokens (equity tokens or a separate token cap table) so grants, vesting and transfers are tracked and automated via smart contracts.
- **Verantwortungseigentum (steward-ownership)** — A German/European ownership philosophy ('ownership of responsibility') in which a company has no profit-extracting external shareholders: control is held by stewards inside the business who have no personal claim on profits, and the company effectively owns itself in service of its purpose.
- **Warrants granted to staff / advisors (worker warrants)** — A company-issued right to buy shares at a set price for a long period, occasionally granted to workers/advisors as compensation — an atypical use of an instrument normally reserved for investors and lenders.
- **Worker cooperative conversion (acquisition-conversion)** — Selling a business to its employees as a worker cooperative, giving worker-owners direct ownership, democratic governance, and annual profit-sharing (patronage).


## Broad-based / ERISA qualified defined-contribution plan

### Profit-Sharing Plan (including 401(k) profit-sharing)  ·  ⚑ likely unfamiliar to a generalist VC

- **What it is:** A qualified retirement plan into which the employer makes discretionary contributions out of company profits to employees' retirement accounts; when bolted onto a 401(k), employees can also defer their own wages.
- **How it works:** Employers decide each year whether and how much to contribute (no required minimum), but must use a set allocation formula (e.g., pro-rata by pay, age-weighted, new comparability/cross-tested, integrated, or flat dollar). Contributions are tax-deductible and can be subject to a vesting schedule, with unvested amounts forfeited if the employee leaves early. Only compensation up to the annual IRC 401(a)(17) limit may be counted.
- **Legal regime / geography:** United States. ERISA + IRC Section 401(a); IRS profit-sharing plan rules. The 2026 401(a)(17) compensation limit is $360,000 (up from $350,000 in 2025), per IRS Notice 2025-67.
- **Who uses it:** Employers of all sizes, frequently small and mid-size businesses, as a flexible retirement benefit; very common as the employer-contribution side of a 401(k).
- **Example:** Southwest Airlines' ProfitSharing Plan is one of its two qualified defined-contribution plans; the profit-sharing contribution is set at the board's discretion, defaulting to an amount equal to 15% of the company's adjusted operating profit, funded to employees' Retirement Savings Plan accounts (SEC-filed plan document).  
  Source: https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/92380/000119312509015591/dex107.htm
- **Notes:** Retirement-plan mechanic outside the cap-table world → TRUE. The $360,000 figure is the statutory indexed compensation cap, verified against IRS Notice 2025-67 (https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/n-25-67.pdf), not estimated.


## Broad-based equity sharing (PE/portco)

### Broad-based equity / carry sharing with portfolio-company employees (Blackstone, KKR)  ·  ⚑ likely unfamiliar to a generalist VC

- **What it is:** Large buyout firms granting equity (or value tied to the firm's investment return) to ALL employees of an acquired portfolio company, not just senior management.
- **How it works:** On control acquisitions, the PE firm grants broad-based ownership interests to the workforce as a free, incremental benefit on top of wages. Employees employed at the time the firm sells or IPOs the company receive a payout in an amount tied to the firm's return on the investment. Mechanically this is often implemented via grants of profits interests in GP/holding entities or via phantom-equity bonus pools that track the deal's value or the carried interest. Adoption is rising: the share of PE-backed LLCs issuing equity to non-management employees rose from 25% (2021) to 36% (2023).
- **Legal regime / geography:** US private markets. Implemented through equity grants, profits interests, or phantom/synthetic equity inside portfolio companies; not a statutory program.
- **Who uses it:** KKR (pioneered by Pete Stavros), Blackstone, and a growing set of buyout firms; per NCEO ~19 PE firms had adopted broad-based ownership pledges.
- **Example:** Blackstone committed to broad-based employee ownership on all new control deals; its Copeland HVAC business (acquired 2023 for ~$14B) is set to distribute equity to its ~18,000 employees.  
  Source: https://www.nceo.org/employee-ownership-blog/private-equity-giant-blackstone-commits-employee-stock-grants
- **Notes:** 'Sharing carry with portfolio-company employees' in practice is usually equity/phantom-equity grants tied to deal returns rather than literal carried-interest allocations; the carried-interest-style mechanism (profits interests / GP-tracking phantom pools) is described in PE deskbook/legal sources.

### Ownership Works (shared-ownership nonprofit; KKR/Pete Stavros model)  ·  ⚑ likely unfamiliar to a generalist VC

- **What it is:** A nonprofit that helps private-equity firms and companies give ALL employees (not just executives) an equity stake in the companies they work for, so workers share in value creation at exit.
- **How it works:** Ownership Works partners with investors, companies, banks, pension funds and labor advocates to structure broad-based equity plans inside portfolio companies. Equity is granted on top of existing wages/benefits at no cost to the worker; employees receive a payout when the company is sold or goes public (or on performance milestones). The nonprofit supports four pillars: structuring/implementation, ownership culture, financial inclusion/resilience, and data/insight. Stated mission: 'Create $20 Billion of Wealth in 10 Years.'
- **Legal regime / geography:** US (and used internationally by partner firms). Equity grants/synthetic equity inside private portfolio companies; not a statutory program.
- **Who uses it:** Founded and chaired by KKR's Pete Stavros; partners include private-equity firms, investment banks, pension funds and LPs deploying broad-based ownership in their deals.
- **Example:** Ownership Works cites Ingersoll Rand making roughly 21,000 employees owners, and C.H.I. Overhead Doors extending ownership to all ~800 employees.  
  Source: https://ownershipworks.org/
- **Notes:** Ingersoll Rand employee count cited as ~21,000 on ownershipworks.org (some press summaries say 16,000); used the org's own figure. The widely cited '$800M of wealth at Ingersoll Rand' figure appears in press summaries, not the org's homepage, so it is omitted here.


## Broad-based ownership / ERISA qualified retirement plan

### Employee Stock Ownership Plan (ESOP)  ·  ⚑ likely unfamiliar to a generalist VC

- **What it is:** A tax-qualified retirement plan, held in a trust, that is designed to invest primarily in the stock of the sponsoring employer, giving employees an ownership stake in the company they work for, typically at no out-of-pocket cost.
- **How it works:** The company sets up an ESOP trust and contributes (or sells) company shares to it, or the trust borrows money to buy shares (ESOPs are the only retirement plan allowed to borrow to acquire employer securities). Shares are allocated to individual employee accounts, usually based on pay or service, and vest over time. When employees retire or leave, the company/trust buys back their vested shares for cash. Per NCEO, employees incur no out-of-pocket expense to participate.
- **Legal regime / geography:** United States. Governed by ERISA (1974) and the Internal Revenue Code: IRC section 401(a) (qualified plan), 4975(e)(8) (qualifying employer securities), 409(p) and 409(h). The IRS and Department of Labor share jurisdiction over ESOP features.
- **Who uses it:** Predominantly privately held US companies (closely held businesses, often as a succession/exit vehicle for retiring owners); a smaller number of public companies. NCEO reports 5,993 private and 418 public companies sponsor ESOPs.
- **Example:** Publix Super Markets — its ESOP, started in 1974, is one of the largest in the country and may be the largest private-company ESOP; the company gives stock to employees who work 1,000+ hours per year at no cost, with workforce over 250,000. King Arthur Baking became 100% employee-owned via ESOP in 2004.  
  Source: https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/employee-stock-ownership-plans-esops
- **Notes:** Trust-based, retirement-law construct that sits outside the startup cap-table world a unicorn VC lives in. Scale data (NCEO, 2023 Form 5500 data, 'Employee Ownership by the Numbers'): 6,609 plans identified as ESOPs at 6,411 companies, 15.1 million participants (of whom 10.9 million active), holding total assets of over $2 trillion. NCEO's separate national ESOP database (plan year 2024 filings) lists 6,422 ESOPs — figures differ by source/year, both cited here rather than averaged. Distinct from an Employee Ownership Trust (EOT), which is a perpetual-trust ownership model, not a qualified retirement plan.


## Broad-based stock purchase plan

### Employee Stock Purchase Plan (ESPP) — Section 423 qualified

- **What it is:** A formal plan that lets employees set aside money through after-tax payroll deductions over an offering period to buy company stock at a discount (up to 15% off fair market value under a tax-qualified Section 423 plan), often with a 'look-back' that prices the purchase off the lower of the offering-date or purchase-date price.
- **How it works:** The employer announces an offering period (commonly 6 or 24 months). Employees elect a payroll-deduction percentage (typically 1–15% of pay); accumulated funds buy stock on the purchase date at the discounted price. For a qualified plan there is no tax at purchase; on sale, a 'qualifying disposition' (held 2+ years from grant and 1+ year from purchase) limits ordinary income to the lesser of actual gain or the grant-date discount, with the rest as long-term capital gain. IRS limits purchases to $25,000 of stock per calendar year.
- **Legal regime / geography:** United States. IRC Section 423 ('qualified'/statutory). Treated as a statutory stock option under IRS Topic No. 427. Section 423 generally requires broad inclusion of employees (e.g., all employees with 2 years of service, with limited exclusions).
- **Who uses it:** Publicly traded companies primarily (the plan needs a liquid market price); a very common broad-based benefit at large public employers.
- **Example:** Adobe's ESPP lets employees set aside up to 25% of compensation to buy Adobe stock every six months at a price at least 15% below fair market value, with the discounted price locked in for a 24-month period.  
  Source: https://benefits.adobe.com/us/financial/employee-stock-purchase-plan-espp
- **Notes:** Well-known public-company benefit a VC would recognize at least loosely. Non-Section-423 ('non-qualified') ESPPs also exist with fewer restrictions and no special tax treatment. NCEO estimates 11.8 million US employees participate in equity-comp plans including restricted stock, options, and ESPPs (2023 data).


## Community/cooperative ownership transition

### Exit to Community (E2C)  ·  ⚑ likely unfamiliar to a generalist VC

- **What it is:** An alternative startup 'exit' in which a company transitions from investor ownership to ownership by its community of stakeholders — employees, users, or customers — instead of an IPO or acquisition.
- **How it works:** Rather than exiting via sale to a strategic/financial buyer or IPO, the company transfers ownership, decision-making and profits to a broader group. There is no single template: it can be a conversion to a cooperative, a trust, or (for crypto-native firms) a DAO with tokens distributed to contributors and users. A core constraint: companies that raised large amounts of VC generally cannot 'exit to community' affordably, so it best fits firms with limited outside capital. Developed as a research/practice project by MEDLab (Nathan Schneider, CU Boulder) and the founder co-op Zebras Unite.
- **Legal regime / geography:** US/global; varies by chosen vehicle (cooperative statutes, trust law, or token/DAO frameworks). No dedicated statute — it is a framework, not a legal form.
- **Who uses it:** Startup founders (often women/underrepresented founders via Zebras Unite) seeking non-extractive exits; community/user-owned ventures.
- **Example:** MEDLab (Nathan Schneider) and Zebras Unite established the Exit to Community project; cited illustrative case is blockchain startup Gitcoin distributing tokens to contributors as a community exit.  
  Source: https://www.colorado.edu/lab/medlab/exit-to-community
- **Notes:** Concept and Gitcoin/DAO example from MEDLab/PitchBook coverage; E2C is a framework with multiple possible legal vehicles rather than a single mechanism.

### Worker cooperative conversion (acquisition-conversion)  ·  ⚑ likely unfamiliar to a generalist VC

- **What it is:** Selling a business to its employees as a worker cooperative, giving worker-owners direct ownership, democratic governance, and annual profit-sharing (patronage).
- **How it works:** A retiring/exiting owner sells the business to a worker cooperative formed by employees. Worker-owners pay a small equity buy-in; because employees rarely have enough savings, the buyout is financed with outside capital (community/cooperative lenders, banks, PE). Profits are distributed as patronage based on labor contributed, and governance is one-member-one-vote. Intermediaries run 'acquisition-conversion' programs to scale this as a succession solution. Project Equity and Shared Capital Cooperative launched 'Accelerate Employee Ownership' (2019) to support such sales.
- **Legal regime / geography:** US. State cooperative-corporation statutes; financed by mission lenders/CDFIs and cooperative capital; can use Section 1042-style rollovers in some structures.
- **Who uses it:** Small-business owners using it as a retirement/succession plan; employees becoming worker-owners; facilitated by Project Equity, Democracy at Work Institute, and cooperative lenders.
- **Example:** Project Equity and Shared Capital Cooperative launched the 'Accelerate Employee Ownership' initiative (2019) supporting owner-to-employee cooperative conversions; Project Equity and the Democracy at Work Institute publish business-conversion case studies.  
  Source: https://project-equity.org/learn/types-of-employee-ownership/worker-cooperatives/
- **Notes:** Mechanics and the Accelerate Employee Ownership initiative from Project Equity and Institute for the Cooperative Economy materials.


## Compensation strategy / plan-design category (option or RSU based)

### Broad-based / all-employee equity grants

- **What it is:** An equity-compensation design in which stock options or RSUs are granted to ALL employees — including part-time and hourly workers — rather than reserved for executives and senior staff.
- **How it works:** Mechanically these use the same instruments as above (ISOs/NSOs/RSUs and their tax rules); the distinguishing feature is breadth of eligibility — front-line and hourly workers participate. Vesting and tax follow the underlying instrument (e.g., RSU value taxed as wages at vest).
- **Legal regime / geography:** United States. Tax/securities treatment follows the underlying instrument (IRC 422 for ISOs, 83 for NSOs/RSUs, 423 for ESPPs).
- **Who uses it:** Companies that deliberately extend ownership widely — historically pioneered in retail/services and now common in tech.
- **Example:** Starbucks' 'Bean Stock,' launched in 1991, was one of the first programs in retail to offer equity to part-time workers; today it grants RSUs to both part-time and full-time 'partners' (Starbucks' term for all employees), vesting over two years.  
  Source: https://about.starbucks.com/history/from-employees-to-partners/
- **Notes:** The concept (broad option/RSU grants) is familiar to a VC, hence FALSE; but the all-hourly-worker version is a distinct, notable category for a 'worker value-sharing' atlas. The official Starbucks page returned HTTP 403 to the fetch tool but is the correct primary source; details corroborated via secondary coverage.


## Conversion process (not a single legal vehicle)

### Employee-Ownership Conversion  ·  ⚑ likely unfamiliar to a generalist VC

- **What it is:** The PROCESS of transitioning a business from conventional (founder/family/investor) ownership to employee ownership - selling or transferring the business to its workers - executed through one of several end-state vehicles: an ESOP, a worker cooperative, or an employee ownership trust.
- **How it works:** Typically begins with a feasibility study estimating the company's value and assessing whether the cash flow can support a buyout. An attorney structures the chosen vehicle. The owner is paid via (a) company cash, (b) a third-party (bank) loan, or (c) a seller note paid over time (a leveraged transaction). Structures range from partial to full sale. The conversion can produce an ESOP (qualified retirement-plan ownership with individual accounts), a worker co-op (democratic membership ownership), or an EOT (collective trust ownership).
- **Legal regime / geography:** United States primarily; the legal regime depends on the chosen end vehicle - ERISA/IRC for an ESOP, state cooperative statute + IRC Subchapter T for a co-op, or trust law for a trust-based structure. Federal policy (e.g. the Main Street Employee Ownership Act) and state employee-ownership centers support conversions.
- **Who uses it:** Retiring/exiting owners seeking succession that preserves jobs, legacy and local control rather than a trade sale or PE buyout; facilitated by employee-ownership centers (e.g. Democracy at Work Institute / US Federation of Worker Cooperatives, Rocky Mountain Employee Ownership Center).
- **Example:** Rocky Mountain Employee Ownership Center (RMEOC) runs an 'Employee Ownership Conversion' service guiding owners through transition to worker cooperative, ESOP, or employee ownership trust structures.  
  Source: https://www.rmeoc.org/services/employee-ownership-conversion/
- **Notes:** This is the PROCESS, not a vehicle - deliberately cross-referenced to the ESOP, worker-cooperative and EOT entries (its possible end-states). Borderline on the unknown rubric: a generalist VC understands 'selling a company,' but the specific employee-ownership conversion pathways, feasibility-study workflow, and seller-note/leveraged financing into an ESOP/co-op/trust are specialized impact-finance practice, so judged likely-unknown. Some quoted sustainability statistics circulating in coverage (e.g. survival-rate comparisons) were not verified against primary studies and are intentionally excluded from claims here.


## Cooperative (member-owned, democratic)

### Worker Cooperative

- **What it is:** A business owned and democratically controlled by its own workers, where each worker-owner has an equal stake and an equal vote (one member, one vote) regardless of capital contribution, and profits are returned to members based on their labor contribution rather than capital invested.
- **How it works:** Workers buy a membership stake to become worker-owners; they elect the board of directors on a one-person-one-vote basis, and the board hires management. Surplus (profit) is distributed to members as patronage allocations based on factors like hours worked, seniority, position and pay. Governance is democratic and ownership is tied to employment - members cannot retain ownership after leaving, and the selling owner of a converted firm cannot keep the stock they sold.
- **Legal regime / geography:** Global; rooted in International Cooperative Alliance principles. In the US, formation is under state cooperative statutes (and some states have specific worker-cooperative or LLC-based statutes); tax treatment under IRC Subchapter T for patronage. Internationally exemplified by Spanish/Basque models.
- **Who uses it:** Workforces seeking democratic ownership; common in home care, food, retail and services. Mondragon (Spain) is the largest worker-cooperative group in the world (employs over 80,000 people); Cooperative Home Care Associates (South Bronx) is the largest US worker cooperative.
- **Example:** Cooperative Home Care Associates (CHCA), founded in the South Bronx, is the largest worker cooperative in the US (roughly 2,000 workers). Employees can buy a $1,000 ownership stake via weekly payroll deductions of $3.65, receive annual dividends based on profitability, and elect 8 of the 13 board members.  
  Source: https://community-wealth.org/content/cooperative-home-care-associates
- **Notes:** Worker cooperative is a broadly recognized ownership form a generalist VC would know by name, though the one-member-one-vote governance and Subchapter T patronage mechanics are specialized. CHCA size figures and Mondragon's 80,000+ headcount are from the cited community-wealth.org / institute.coop summaries; treat headcounts as approximate as they were not pulled from an audited primary filing.


## Equity instrument for workers

### Profits interest (LLC equity grant / carry-style worker equity)  ·  ⚑ likely unfamiliar to a generalist VC

- **What it is:** An LLC/partnership equity grant giving an employee a share of the company's FUTURE profits and appreciation above a grant-date threshold, with no claim on existing value.
- **How it works:** A profits interest entitles the holder only to value created after the grant: a 'threshold/hurdle' is set at the company's FMV on the grant date, and the holder shares in profits, distributions and appreciation above it. Structured under the IRS safe harbor (Rev. Proc. 93-27), the grant is not taxable as compensation when made because the future-growth interest has no readily determinable value. It is treated as true equity for tax, enabling capital-gains rates on later sale/redemption. A key consequence: the recipient legally becomes a partner and is issued a K-1 instead of a W-2 (you cannot be both employee and partner in the same partnership for tax).
- **Legal regime / geography:** US. LLC/partnership tax law; IRS Rev. Proc. 93-27 safe harbor; capital-gains treatment on disposition.
- **Who uses it:** LLC-structured startups and partnerships granting equity to key employees; conceptually the same family as the 'carried interest' GPs receive in funds.
- **Example:** not public  
  Source: https://carta.com/learn/startups/compensation/equity-incentive-plans/profits-interest/
- **Notes:** Definition/mechanics/tax from Carta and The Tax Adviser. Closely related to carried interest (same instrument family); included because it is the technical vehicle most often used to share carry-like upside with operating-company workers. example_source_url points to the Carta definitional source rather than a named company.

### Warrants granted to staff / advisors (worker warrants)  ·  ⚑ likely unfamiliar to a generalist VC

- **What it is:** A company-issued right to buy shares at a set price for a long period, occasionally granted to workers/advisors as compensation — an atypical use of an instrument normally reserved for investors and lenders.
- **How it works:** A warrant is issued directly by the company and gives the holder the right to purchase shares at a fixed strike price, typically with long expirations (5-15 years) and flexible, negotiable terms. The DEFAULT use is as a 'deal sweetener' for investors, banks or lenders (attached to venture debt, convertible notes, equipment financing) and is usually fully vested at issuance. The atypical, frontier application is granting warrants to employees, advisors or consultants as equity-style compensation; unlike options (which must be struck at/above the 409A FMV), warrants can be struck below FMV with the discount reported as compensation income, and they are often transferable.
- **Legal regime / geography:** US. Securities/contract law; when granted as compensation, the discount is taxable compensation income; not subject to the same 409A FMV constraint as options.
- **Who uses it:** Primarily investors/lenders; the worker/advisor-compensation use is the unusual case (advisor warrants, vendor/partner warrants), which is why it sits at the frontier rather than as standard staff comp.
- **Example:** not public  
  Source: https://carta.com/learn/equity/stock-options-vs-warrants/
- **Notes:** Per the sources, options (not warrants) are the standard employee tool; warrants are typically investor/lender instruments. Presented honestly as the atypical worker-comp application. Mechanics/definitions corroborated across Carta, EquityList and Lighter Capital search results; the Carta page returned 403 on direct fetch but the definition is consistent across multiple results; no named worker-warrant company example verified, so real_example left as 'not public.'


## Executive / select-group deferred compensation

### Nonqualified Deferred Compensation (NQDC) — top-hat plans, SERPs, deferred comp  ·  ⚑ likely unfamiliar to a generalist VC

- **What it is:** A legally binding right to compensation that is earned in one year but paid (and taxed) in a later year, offered to a select group of management or highly compensated employees outside the qualified-plan limits.
- **How it works:** An executive (or the employer) defers salary/bonus to a future date; the deferral election must be irrevocable before the year the compensation is earned. Distributions are permitted only on specified 409A events: separation from service, death, disability, change in control, unforeseeable emergency, or a fixed date/schedule. Includes top-hat plans, supplemental executive retirement plans (SERPs), and excess benefit plans. Noncompliance triggers immediate taxation at vesting plus a 20% excise tax and interest.
- **Legal regime / geography:** United States. IRC Section 409A (plus constructive-receipt/economic-benefit doctrines). 'Top-hat' plans for a select group of management/highly compensated employees are exempt from most of ERISA's substantive provisions.
- **Who uses it:** Public and large private companies, for executives and other highly compensated employees only (these plans must be limited to a select group to keep their ERISA exemption).
- **Example:** Costco Wholesale maintains a nonqualified deferred compensation plan for certain highly compensated employees, including its named executive officers, as disclosed in its DEF 14A proxy statement.  
  Source: https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/909832/000119312509255833/ddef14a.htm
- **Notes:** Executive-comp / deferred-comp regime, not a cap-table instrument → TRUE. Nuance: a VC does know 409A in the context of 409A *valuations* (option strike pricing); what is less familiar is Section 409A as the rulebook governing NQDC deferred-compensation plans, which is the sense meant here.


## Foundation-owned enterprise (mission-lock, ownerless)

### Enterprise / Industrial Foundation (Danish Model)  ·  ⚑ likely unfamiliar to a generalist VC

- **What it is:** A foundation that owns and controls an operating company. The foundation is a self-governing, ownerless legal entity with no shareholders to whom it pays dividends; it holds a controlling block of the company (often a minority of share capital but a majority of voting rights) and is actively involved in the business while channeling surplus to research and charitable purposes.
- **How it works:** The founder donates or transfers the company's controlling shares to a foundation, which thereafter cannot be bought or sold and has no private owners. The foundation's board governs the company for the long term and reinvests or distributes profits to scientific, social or charitable causes rather than to individuals. Dual-class share structures often let the foundation keep voting control even while the company is publicly listed (the foundation holds a minority of capital but a majority of votes).
- **Legal regime / geography:** Denmark (and broadly Northern Europe / Germany). Danish industrial foundations (erhvervsdrivende fonde) are defined under Danish law as ownerless entities holding shares in companies. This is the 'Danish model' variant of steward-ownership.
- **Who uses it:** Large founder-built companies seeking permanence and insulation from takeover; enterprise foundations own three of the four largest Danish companies - A.P. Moller-Maersk, Novo Nordisk and Carlsberg. German parallels include the Robert Bosch and Carl Zeiss foundations.
- **Example:** The Novo Nordisk Foundation, via its holding arm Novo Holdings, is the controlling shareholder of pharmaceutical company Novo Nordisk and of Novonesis - holding a minority of share capital but a majority of the voting rights.  
  Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Novo_Nordisk_Foundation
- **Notes:** Added as a well-documented adjacent at the advisor's suggestion - it is the large-corporate, foundation-based implementation of steward-ownership (cross-referenced to the steward-ownership umbrella entry). The Danish/European foundation-ownership model is niche to a US generalist VC, hence likely_unknown=true. Carlsberg Foundation grant figure (DKK 832m in 2022) appeared in search summaries; not independently re-verified on a primary filing, so excluded from the core claim.


## Individual equity — full-value award

### Restricted Stock Award (RSA)

- **What it is:** An outright grant of actual company shares that are subject to vesting and forfeiture restrictions until earned — distinct from RSUs because the recipient holds real shares from the start.
- **How it works:** Shares are issued at grant but can be forfeited if vesting conditions aren't met. By default the value is taxed as ordinary income as the shares vest, but the recipient can file an IRC Section 83(b) election within 30 days of grant to be taxed on the (usually low) value at grant instead, converting future appreciation to capital gain. Commonly used for founders and very early employees when share value is near zero.
- **Legal regime / geography:** United States. IRC Section 83, including the Section 83(b) election.
- **Who uses it:** Founders and earliest employees of startups (when stock is cheap), and some public companies for executives.
- **Example:** NCEO describes restricted stock plans that 'provide employees with the right to purchase shares at fair market value or a discount, or employees may receive shares at no cost,' where 'the shares employees acquire are not really theirs yet' until restrictions lapse.  
  Source: https://www.nceo.org/what-is-employee-ownership/stock-options-restricted-phantom-sars-espps
- **Notes:** Founders' restricted stock + 83(b) election is core early-stage VC knowledge. Distinct from RSUs (real shares + 83(b) available vs. a promise of shares taxed at vest).

### Restricted Stock Unit (RSU)

- **What it is:** A promise from an employer to deliver shares of company stock (or their cash equivalent) on a future date once vesting conditions are met; the employee owns nothing until the units vest.
- **How it works:** RSUs are granted with a vesting schedule (time-based, e.g., 4 years with a 1-year cliff, and/or performance-based). At vesting, the fair market value of the shares is treated as W-2 wages and is subject to income and FICA tax withholding (often handled via sell-to-cover). The employee's basis equals the value taxed at vest; later gain/loss on sale is capital gain/loss. There is no tax at grant and no 83(b) election available (unlike restricted stock awards).
- **Legal regime / geography:** United States. Taxed under IRC Section 83 at vesting; deferred-settlement RSUs can implicate IRC Section 409A.
- **Who uses it:** Public companies (the dominant equity vehicle for large public employers) and late-stage private companies; granted from rank-and-file to executives.
- **Example:** Amazon's primary equity vehicle is the RSU, granted to the majority of eligible employees with a back-loaded vesting schedule (5% year 1, 15% year 2, 40% year 3, 40% year 4); employees working under 30 hours/week are generally ineligible.  
  Source: https://carta.com/learn/equity/rsu/
- **Notes:** Core cap-table instrument familiar to any VC. Cap-table instrument → FALSE.


## Individual equity — partnership/LLC equity (option-analog)

### Profits Interest (LLC) / Management Incentive Units  ·  ⚑ likely unfamiliar to a generalist VC

- **What it is:** A grant of an equity stake in an LLC or partnership that entitles the holder only to a share of FUTURE profits and appreciation above a threshold value set at grant — with no claim on the entity's existing capital — used as the equity-incentive vehicle for businesses taxed as partnerships.
- **How it works:** The recipient gets a partnership/LLC interest tied to growth above a 'threshold value' (analogous to an option strike). Under the IRS safe harbor, a properly structured profits interest is not taxed at grant or at vesting, even without an 83(b) election; on a later sale, gain typically flows through as long-term capital gain. Conditions include receiving it as a partner for services and (generally) holding for two years.
- **Legal regime / geography:** United States. IRS safe harbors under Revenue Procedure 93-27 and Revenue Procedure 2001-43; partnership tax under Subchapter K.
- **Who uses it:** Companies organized as LLCs/partnerships (operating businesses, real estate, private-equity-owned portfolio companies, professional firms) — the equivalent of stock options for the LLC world, where ISOs/RSUs don't fit.
- **Example:** not public — no single named-company example verified to a clean primary source; left unfabricated. Authoritative mechanism basis: Revenue Procedure 93-27 (IRS profits-interest safe harbor).  
  Source: https://www.thetaxadviser.com/issues/2025/jan/profits-interests-the-most-tax-efficient-equity-grant-to-employees/
- **Notes:** A VC fluent in C-corp cap tables may not know the LLC profits-interest mechanism, which is the standard option-analog for partnership-taxed companies → TRUE. real_example not set to a specific company because a single clean, named primary-source example was not verified.


## Individual equity — stock option (nonstatutory)

### Non-Qualified Stock Option (NSO / NQSO)

- **What it is:** A nonstatutory stock option that can be granted to anyone — employees, contractors, advisors, directors — under which the spread between exercise price and fair market value at exercise is taxed as ordinary income.
- **How it works:** The holder exercises by paying the exercise price; the difference between the stock's fair market value at exercise and the price paid is ordinary (wage) income at that time, subject to withholding for employees. Subsequent gain or loss after exercise is capital gain/loss based on the holding period. If the option has no readily determinable value at grant, there is no taxable event at grant.
- **Legal regime / geography:** United States. Nonstatutory option under IRS Topic No. 427; taxed under IRC Section 83. Not subject to the ISO statutory limits.
- **Who uses it:** Startups and public companies; the only option type usable for non-employees (advisors, consultants, board members) and for ISO grants that exceed the $100,000 ISO limit.
- **Example:** IRS Topic No. 427 describes nonstatutory stock options and their taxation; companies routinely grant NSOs to non-employee service providers because ISOs are limited to employees.  
  Source: https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc427
- **Notes:** Core cap-table instrument. Could not source a single named-company NSO-only example as cleanly as ISO; primary IRS guidance cited instead of a vague placeholder.


## Individual equity — stock option (statutory)

### Incentive Stock Option (ISO)

- **What it is:** A statutory stock option, available to employees only, that gives the right to buy company stock at a fixed exercise price and carries preferential federal tax treatment if holding requirements are met.
- **How it works:** An employee is granted the right to buy a stated number of shares at the grant-date price. There is generally no ordinary income tax at grant or exercise; if the shares are held at least 2 years from grant and 1 year from exercise, the entire gain at sale is long-term capital gain. The spread at exercise is, however, an adjustment for the alternative minimum tax (AMT). The aggregate fair market value of stock for which ISOs first become exercisable in any calendar year (measured at grant) cannot exceed $100,000.
- **Legal regime / geography:** United States. IRC Section 422; IRS Topic No. 427 (statutory stock options).
- **Who uses it:** Startups and venture-backed private companies most heavily (the default option type for employees), plus public companies; employees only (not contractors/advisors).
- **Example:** Aegis Identity Software, Inc. adopted an Incentive Stock Option Plan for key employees, with options intended to constitute incentive stock options within the meaning of Section 422 of the Internal Revenue Code (Form S-1 exhibit).  
  Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/422
- **Notes:** Core cap-table instrument every unicorn VC knows. Discriminator applied: cap-table instrument → FALSE.


## Mission-lock governance device (single special share)

### Golden Share / Veto Share Model  ·  ⚑ likely unfamiliar to a generalist VC

- **What it is:** A single special class of share that carries little or no economic value but holds defined VETO rights, used (in the steward-ownership context) to give an independent mission-guardian - typically a foundation - the legal power to block changes that would betray a company's purpose, especially attempts to sell control or to extract profits for private gain.
- **How it works:** The company issues a special 'golden share' (e.g. ~1% of voting rights plus capped or no dividend rights) to a trusted external guardian such as the Purpose Foundation. That share grants a veto over amendments to the articles of association that contradict steward-ownership principles - meaning the company cannot lawfully (a) be sold for the founders'/owners' speculative profit or to outsiders not involved in operations, or (b) distribute profits beyond agreed limits. The veto makes the founder's mission promises legally enforceable and irreversible.
- **Legal regime / geography:** Governance device implemented within ordinary corporate/company law (here, German company law for Ecosia); the steward-ownership 'golden/veto share' meaning is distinct from the sovereign-state 'golden share' used in privatizations - that political/sovereign sense is off-thesis and only noted for disambiguation.
- **Who uses it:** Startups and growth companies adopting steward-ownership without a foundation owning the whole company; the guardian role is often held by the Purpose Foundation.
- **Example:** Ecosia (the search engine that funds tree planting) converted to steward-ownership in 2018 and granted a 'Golden Share' to the Purpose Foundation, with ~1% of voting rights and a veto against article changes that breach steward-ownership principles. Founder Christian Kroll: 'Now Ecosia can never be sold - and profits will never be extracted from the company.'  
  Source: https://blog.ecosia.org/trees-not-profits/
- **Notes:** A generalist VC likely knows the WORD 'golden share' (privatization context) but probably NOT the steward-ownership veto-share usage - that specific application is niche, hence likely_unknown=true. This is one implementation route for steward-ownership (umbrella entry). Caution: do not confuse with sovereign/government golden shares in state-owned-enterprise privatizations, which are a different mechanism.


## Mission-lock ownership umbrella / governance philosophy

### Steward-Ownership (Verantwortungseigentum)  ·  ⚑ likely unfamiliar to a generalist VC

- **What it is:** An ownership model (German term Verantwortungseigentum, 'responsible/responsibility ownership') built on two binding principles: (1) self-determination/self-governance - voting control stays with people connected to the company's mission and the company cannot become an object of speculation; and (2) purpose-orientation - profits are a means to an end, are reinvested or used for the mission/common good, and cannot be extracted for owners' personal enrichment ('asset lock').
- **How it works:** Steward-ownership is an umbrella implemented through several legal vehicles depending on jurisdiction: enterprise/industrial foundations (the 'Danish model'), trust ownership (e.g. perpetual purpose trusts), or - for startups - a golden/veto-share held by a mission-guardian foundation. Control shares carry voting rights but limited or no economic rights; economic (dividend) rights are capped or directed to mission/charity; and legally binding, typically irreversible restrictions prevent selling the company at a speculative profit or extracting profits.
- **Legal regime / geography:** German/European origin; the term was coined by Germany's Purpose Foundation (Purpose Stiftung) in 2017. It is a global movement implemented via existing legal forms in each jurisdiction (German GmbH + golden share, US purpose trusts, Danish foundations, UK trusts). Advocates (e.g. Stiftung Verantwortungseigentum, founded 2019) have pushed for a dedicated German legal form.
- **Who uses it:** Mission-driven companies wanting permanent independence: historically Robert Bosch and Carl Zeiss (Germany); modern examples include Ecosia, Bosch, Novo Nordisk (Denmark), Buurtzorg (Netherlands), John Lewis Partnership (UK) and Patagonia (USA).
- **Example:** Bosch and Zeiss are the foundational examples: after Carl Zeiss died, co-owner Ernst Abbe established the Carl Zeiss Foundation in 1889 and donated the entire business to it, so the company cannot be sold and profits are reinvested or used for the common good; Robert Bosch similarly transferred his company into a foundation structure.  
  Source: https://purpose-economy.org/en/whats-steward-ownership/
- **Notes:** This is the conceptual UMBRELLA; the PPT, golden/veto share, and enterprise foundation entries are its specific implementation mechanisms - cross-referenced deliberately. The German term and the movement framing are strongly European and niche, so likely unknown to a US generalist VC. Distinct from a worker cooperative (steward-ownership is about mission-lock and control-by-stewards, not necessarily democratic worker control).


## Qualified-retirement-plan ownership (US)

### Leveraged ESOP Buyout

- **What it is:** A US Employee Stock Ownership Plan (ESOP) is a tax-qualified retirement plan that buys shares of the sponsoring employer and credits them to individual employee accounts; in a *leveraged* ESOP the trust borrows money to buy a large block of stock (often the full company) at once, then repays the loan over time out of company contributions, releasing shares to employees as the debt is paid down.
- **How it works:** The company obtains bank financing and re-lends the same amount to the ESOP trust as an 'acquisition loan' (an IRC Section 4975(d)(3) exempt loan). The ESOP uses the proceeds to buy employer stock, which is held in a suspense account pledged as collateral. Annual tax-deductible company contributions repay the loan; as it is repaid, shares are 'released' from suspense and allocated to employee accounts. Selling shareholders of a C corporation who sell at least 30% to the ESOP, having held the stock 3+ years, can defer capital-gains tax under IRC Section 1042 by reinvesting in qualified replacement property.
- **Legal regime / geography:** United States. ERISA-qualified plan (Employee Retirement Income Security Act); Internal Revenue Code Sections 4975(d)(3) (exempt loan), 1042 (capital-gains deferral), 415 (contribution limits); DOL oversight, annual Form 5500 filing.
- **Who uses it:** US private-company owners using it as a succession/exit vehicle, especially profitable mid-size firms. ESOP guidance bodies note a practical floor of roughly 25 employees and ~$1M EBITDA. Publix Super Markets is the largest US example.
- **Example:** Publix Super Markets, Inc. is the largest employee-owned company in the US via its PROFIT Plan ESOP, with over 200,000 participating associates; it acquires shares of the company and credits them to individual employee accounts.  
  Source: https://www.nceo.org/research/employee-ownership-100-largest-employee-owned-companies
- **Notes:** ESOP is mainstream US corporate-finance/exit vocabulary a generalist VC would recognize. The leverage mechanics, IRC 1042 deferral, and DOL/ERISA fiduciary framework are the specialized parts. 'Employee-ownership conversion' (separate entry) is the *process* that produces an ESOP; this entry is the *vehicle*. Publix participation figure ('over 200,000') is from search summary of NCEO; the exact number was not independently confirmed on a fetched primary filing, so treat the headcount as approximate.


## Synthetic equity (appreciation-only)

### Stock Appreciation Right (SAR)  ·  ⚑ likely unfamiliar to a generalist VC

- **What it is:** A right to receive the monetary equivalent of the increase in the value of a specified number of company shares over a set period, with no upfront purchase required; payable in cash, in shares, or a combination.
- **How it works:** If the stock is worth $10 at grant and $18 at exercise, the holder receives $8 per SAR unit. SARs vest and can often be exercised any time after vesting. They are sometimes paired with options ('tandem SARs') to help fund the exercise or taxes. The value received (minus any consideration paid) is ordinary income to the employee at exercise and deductible by the employer. Cash-settled SARs avoid dilution; stock-settled SARs dilute at exercise.
- **Legal regime / geography:** United States. Like phantom stock, generally governed by IRC Section 409A; taxed as ordinary income at exercise.
- **Who uses it:** Private companies (a leading non-ESOP ownership alternative) and some public companies; used to give upside without requiring an employee cash outlay.
- **Example:** Intertape Polymer Group maintains a 2012 Stock Appreciation Rights Plan under which SARs are granted to employees and non-employee directors (SEC-filed plan document).  
  Source: https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/880224/000119312516524772/d174071dex42.htm
- **Notes:** Synthetic-equity construct → TRUE. NCEO notes SARs are economically like phantom stock but tied to appreciation only.


## Synthetic equity (cash-settled, no real shares)

### Phantom Stock  ·  ⚑ likely unfamiliar to a generalist VC

- **What it is:** A contractual promise to pay a future cash (or stock) bonus equal to the value of a set number of notional company shares — or the increase in that value — without issuing any real equity, voting rights, or ownership.
- **How it works:** The company grants 'phantom units' under an agreement; they vest over time or on milestones, and pay out in cash at a defined triggering event (commonly a sale of the company, separation, retirement, death, or disability). At payout the amount is ordinary income to the employee with income and FICA withholding, and the company takes a corresponding deduction. There is no dilution and no transfer of control.
- **Legal regime / geography:** United States. Treated as nonqualified deferred compensation governed by IRC Section 409A, which restricts payout to permissible triggering events.
- **Who uses it:** Privately held companies (especially those that don't want to dilute ownership or deal with securities/cap-table complexity) rewarding key employees; common at family businesses and LLCs.
- **Example:** not public — no single named-company phantom-stock arrangement verified to a clean primary source; left unfabricated per anti-hallucination rules. Authoritative mechanism description: NCEO, 'Phantom Stock and Stock Appreciation Rights (SARs).'  
  Source: https://www.nceo.org/articles/phantom-stock-appreciation-rights-sars
- **Notes:** Synthetic-equity, private-company tool outside the VC norm → TRUE. real_example deliberately not set to a specific company because phantom-stock arrangements at named public companies are rarely cleanly disclosed; per anti-hallucination rules a value was not invented.


## Synthetic/phantom equity

### Phantom stock and Stock Appreciation Rights (SARs) — synthetic equity

- **What it is:** Cash (or stock) bonus arrangements that pay employees an amount tied to share value, without giving them actual legal ownership of equity.
- **How it works:** Phantom stock is 'a promise to pay a bonus in the form of the equivalent of either the value of company shares or the increase in that value over a period of time.' A SAR 'is much like phantom stock, except it provides the right to the monetary equivalent of the increase in the value of a specified number of shares over a specified period.' Full-value phantom stock mirrors an RSU payout; appreciation-only phantom stock works like a SAR. A grant agreement sets the baseline value, vesting schedule and triggering/payout event. Employees are taxed when the right is exercised; the value (minus consideration paid) is ordinary income to the employee and deductible by the employer.
- **Legal regime / geography:** US. Non-qualified, non-tax-qualified deferred-compensation arrangements; per advisory sources subject to IRC Section 409A (NQDC rules) with payouts taxed as ordinary income; can be deemed de facto ERISA plans if broadly offered and deferred past termination.
- **Who uses it:** Private companies (and LLCs/closely held firms) that want to give equity-like upside without diluting ownership or sharing control; common where actual stock grants are impractical.
- **Example:** not public  
  Source: https://www.nceo.org/articles/phantom-stock-appreciation-rights-sars
- **Notes:** Definitions/mechanics and tax treatment from NCEO. NCEO's article does NOT itself discuss Section 409A; the 409A / non-qualified-deferred-comp attribution comes from advisory sources (Plancorp, Sofer Advisors). Marked likely_unknown=false because phantom stock/SARs are standard equity-comp tools a finance generalist would recognize; included as the synthetic-equity substrate that the frontier mechanisms build on. ## Tokenized ownership

### Equity crowdfunding participation by employees (Regulation Crowdfunding)  ·  ⚑ likely unfamiliar to a generalist VC

- **What it is:** Using the securities-crowdfunding exemption to let a broad base of small investors — potentially including a company's own employees — buy equity in the company.
- **How it works:** Under Regulation Crowdfunding a company can offer and sell securities to the general public (including non-accredited investors) without full SEC registration, up to a capped aggregate amount per 12 months, through a registered funding portal. Anyone can invest, including employees and insiders, subject to per-investor limits keyed to income/net worth for non-accredited investors. Securities are generally restricted from resale for one year, and issuers must disclose insiders and 20%+ beneficial owners. As a worker-ownership tool the idea is to let employees buy in alongside the crowd, though the regime is not designed specifically for employees.
- **Legal regime / geography:** US. SEC Regulation Crowdfunding (17 CFR Part 227), authorized under Title III of the JOBS Act; current maximum raise is $5 million in a 12-month period (per investor.gov).
- **Who uses it:** Early-stage companies raising small-dollar equity from the public via funding portals; employees can participate as ordinary investors.
- **Example:** not public  
  Source: https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/glossary/regulation-crowdfunding
- **Notes:** The $5M Reg CF aggregate cap is confirmed via investor.gov (a US government source); an older $1.07M figure appeared in a search summary but predates the 2021 amendment that raised the cap to $5M. No specific company using Reg CF to make its own employees owners was verified, so real_example is 'not public'; the example_source_url points to the regulatory frame, not a named employer.

### Tokenized employee ownership (equity/token grants on-chain)  ·  ⚑ likely unfamiliar to a generalist VC

- **What it is:** Representing employee ownership stakes as blockchain tokens (equity tokens or a separate token cap table) so grants, vesting and transfers are tracked and automated via smart contracts.
- **How it works:** Ownership is digitally represented on a blockchain; equity tokens can carry the same rights as shares (dividends, voting, liquidation preference). Vesting schedules and grant logic are enforced by smart contracts rather than spreadsheets, automating issuance, release and compliance. Crypto-native companies commonly run TWO cap tables — an equity cap table and a token cap table — with allocations carved out for founders, employees (including option-pool/retention grants), treasury and investors. The result is a real-time, programmable shareholder/owner registry.
- **Legal regime / geography:** US/global and evolving; tokenized securities still fall under securities law (the token does not change the underlying legal nature of the equity). Most concrete activity to date is in crypto-native firms via dual equity/token structures.
- **Who uses it:** Crypto/web3-native startups and token projects; cap-table tooling vendors (e.g., Pulley, Eqvista) market token-cap-table software.
- **Example:** not public  
  Source: not public
- **Notes:** Concept and dual-cap-table mechanics from search results (Tomasz Tunguz, Eqvista, Pulley). The only 'company' example found (FasterCapital's 'Tech Innovators Inc.') is a fabricated content-marketing illustration, so it is deliberately NOT cited; real_example and example_source_url set to 'not public' per anti-hallucination rule.


## Trust-based all-employee ownership (UK statutory)

### Employee Ownership Trust (EOT)  ·  ⚑ likely unfamiliar to a generalist VC

- **What it is:** A UK trust that holds a permanent or long-term controlling shareholding in a company on behalf of ALL the company's employees collectively, rather than allocating shares to employees individually. Modeled conceptually on the John Lewis Partnership (which predates the statute) and given a statutory tax definition by the Finance Act 2014.
- **How it works:** Selling shareholders transfer a controlling stake (more than 50% of voting rights, profits and assets) to the trust. The company typically funds the purchase by paying its profits to the trust over several years (commonly five to seven), which the trust uses to pay the former owners. Employees do not receive individual shares; instead the EOT-controlled company can pay tax-advantaged bonuses. Qualification requires an 'all-employee benefit' requirement and an 'equality requirement' (benefits equal, or varying only by remuneration, length of service, or hours worked).
- **Legal regime / geography:** United Kingdom. Statutory definition created by the Finance Act 2014. Historically the sale of a controlling stake to a qualifying EOT was fully exempt from capital gains tax; from 26 November 2025 this relief was reduced so that 50% of the gain on a qualifying disposal is exempt. Income-tax (not NIC) exemption applies to qualifying employee bonuses up to GBP 3,600 per employee per year.
- **Who uses it:** UK owner-managers seeking succession without a trade sale; now the dominant route in the UK employee-ownership sector (deemed/created EOTs represent more than three-quarters of UK employee-owned companies).
- **Example:** Richer Sounds: founder Julian Richer transferred a 60% stake to a John Lewis-style Employee Ownership Trust in 2019 for the benefit of its 500-plus employees; the company paid him an initial GBP 9.2m, of which GBP 3.5m was passed to staff (GBP 1,000 per year of service).  
  Source: https://www.lawblacks.com/2019/05/22/the-richer-sounds-of-employee-ownership-trusts/
- **Notes:** Distinct from the US ESOP: an EOT holds shares collectively in trust for all employees and does NOT distribute individual equity. A separate US state-trust 'EOT'/perpetual-purpose variant is emerging - do not conflate it with the UK Finance Act 2014 statutory regime. UK-specific trust/tax structure is likely outside a US generalist VC's working vocabulary. Other UK EOT examples include Aardman Animations and Riverford Organic (named in general EO coverage).


## Trust-based mission-lock ownership (US, non-charitable)

### Perpetual Purpose Trust (PPT)  ·  ⚑ likely unfamiliar to a generalist VC

- **What it is:** A non-charitable trust created to advance a defined PURPOSE (a mission) rather than to benefit named individuals, and which may continue indefinitely. When it owns a company's voting shares, the trust effectively takes the seat of a shareholder; because a trust is not a person, profits cannot be extracted for any individual's personal gain.
- **How it works:** The company's voting (control) shares are transferred to the trust, which is governed by a trustee or trust stewardship/protector committee of representative stakeholders charged with upholding the stated purpose. The trust never seeks to exit, so control cannot be sold and the company's mission is locked in even as leaders change; economic value is directed toward the trust's defined social/environmental purpose or to stakeholders rather than to outside owners.
- **Legal regime / geography:** United States; relies on state non-charitable purpose trust law (permitted in many US states; the Patagonia trust used a Delaware-permitted structure and the OGC trust was set up as an Oregon Benefit Company). Not a charity, so it lacks the tax exemptions and donor-deduction features of charitable foundations.
- **Who uses it:** Founders and owners seeking permanent mission protection and independence (a US-law route to steward-ownership). Used by Patagonia (in part) and by mid-size mission-driven firms such as Organically Grown Company and Biohabitats.
- **Example:** In 2018 Organically Grown Company (one of the largest US independent organic produce distributors) created the Sustainable Food and Agriculture Perpetual Purpose Trust (SFAPPT), set up as an Oregon Benefit Company, and transferred majority ownership to the trust, which 'will never exit.'  
  Source: https://sustainablefoodandagtrust.com/our-story
- **Notes:** The PPT is the US legal *device* most often used to implement steward-ownership (separate umbrella entry) and is a component of Patagonia's structure (the Patagonia Purpose Trust). Non-charitable purpose-trust law is niche even among US lawyers, so it is likely outside a generalist VC's vocabulary. Distinct from a charitable foundation (no charitable tax status) and from the UK EOT (no all-employee-benefit statutory test).


## Worker-ownership buyout fund

### Apis & Heritage Capital Partners — Employee-Led Buyout (ELBO)  ·  ⚑ likely unfamiliar to a generalist VC

- **What it is:** A mission-driven fund that finances the purchase of profitable companies from retiring owners and converts them to 100% employee ownership, explicitly to close the racial wealth gap.

## broad-based employee share scheme

### All-employee tax-advantaged share schemes (UK SIP / SAYE)

- **What it is:** Two UK government-approved schemes (Share Incentive Plan and Save As You Earn / Sharesave) that let companies give or sell shares to all employees on similar terms with income-tax and National-Insurance advantages - value-sharing through real share ownership but without transferring control.
- **How it works:** Under a Share Incentive Plan (SIP), shares are held in an HMRC-approved trust; employers can give up to GBP 3,600 of free shares a year, employees can buy up to GBP 1,800 (or 10% of salary) of partnership shares from pre-tax pay, employers can add up to 2 matching shares per partnership share, and dividends can buy further shares; if kept in the plan 5 years, no income tax or NI is due on their value. Under SAYE/Sharesave, employees save GBP 5-500 a month for 3 or 5 years and then can buy shares at up to a 20% discount to the grant-date price. Both must be offered broadly to eligible employees.
- **Legal regime / geography:** United Kingdom. HMRC tax-advantaged share schemes; SIP introduced in 2000. These are 'all-employee' schemes (vs discretionary CSOP/EMI).
- **Who uses it:** UK listed and larger private companies wanting broad employee share ownership with tax efficiency; widely used by big employers as a benefit.
- **Example:** The schemes themselves, defined by HMRC/gov.uk: a SIP lets employees keep shares in the plan for 5 years with 'no Income Tax or National Insurance' on their value; SAYE lets employees buy shares at up to 20% below the grant-day price.  
  Source: https://www.gov.uk/tax-employee-share-schemes/share-incentive-plans-sips
- **Notes:** SIP mechanics (free/partnership/matching/dividend shares, GBP 3,600 / GBP 1,800 limits, 5-year hold, income-tax/NI relief) verified directly from gov.uk. Included as the 'value-sharing without control' angle, flagged rather than padded. A generalist VC is likely broadly aware of employee share schemes (option pools/RSUs analog), so likely_unknown_to_ethan = false, though the specific UK SIP/SAYE rules may be less familiar.


## collective capital-sharing fund (historical)

### Lontagarfonderna (Swedish wage-earner funds) - HISTORICAL/ABOLISHED  ·  ⚑ likely unfamiliar to a generalist VC

- **What it is:** A 1980s Swedish system of collective, union-influenced funds intended to gradually shift corporate ownership toward employees/society by channeling a slice of company profits into regional funds that bought shares - a far more limited version of the original radical 'Meidner plan'.
- **How it works:** As enacted (1983), five regional funds were financed by a tax on company profits and on the wage sum; total capitalization was capped (around SEK 17.5 billion) and no single fund could own more than 8% of any company's stock. This watered-down version replaced the original 1975-76 Meidner proposal, which would have forced profitable firms to issue new shares worth 20% of annual profits each year to union-controlled funds until they owned a majority.
- **Legal regime / geography:** Sweden. Enacted by the Social Democratic government in 1983 and ABOLISHED by the incoming centre-right (Bildt) coalition in 1991.
- **Who uses it:** Historical only - no longer in operation. Originally proposed by LO (the blue-collar trade union confederation) and economist Rudolf Meidner; implemented by the Social Democrats.
- **Example:** The Swedish wage-earner funds themselves: introduced 1983 (five regional funds, profit + wage-sum tax financing, max 8% stake per company, ~SEK 17.5bn cap) and dismantled in 1991 by the conservative-led government.  
  Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Employee_funds
- **Notes:** MUST be flagged historical: introduced 1983, abolished 1991. Caps (8% per company, ~SEK 17.5bn) and the original Meidner 20%-of-profits-in-new-shares design verified via the Employee funds article and Meidner-plan secondary sources. Included because it is the canonical large-scale attempt at collective worker capital ownership in the Nordics and is a useful 'what was tried and reversed' contrast.


## collective employee profit-sharing fund

### Henkilostorahasto (Finnish Personnel Fund)  ·  ⚑ likely unfamiliar to a generalist VC

- **What it is:** A Finnish fund owned and controlled by a company's or agency's own personnel that collectively receives the employer's performance/profit bonuses and allocates them to members as personal fund units, functioning as a collective reward and savings mechanism.
- **How it works:** The employer first adopts a performance/profit bonus system; the resulting bonuses are paid into the personnel fund rather than directly to individuals. The fund (owned and managed by employees, who automatically become members through their employment) distributes contributions as personal fund units. Members can withdraw a limited portion (e.g. up to 15%) annually if rules allow, and the balance on leaving employment. Tax advantages apply - employees receive part of fund income tax-free.
- **Legal regime / geography:** Finland. Governed by the Act on Personnel Funds (934/2010). Establishment is voluntary, by agreement between employer and staff; an organisation generally needs at least 5 (some sources cite 10) regularly employed people and a minimum income level.
- **Who uses it:** Finnish companies and public agencies that operate a profit/performance bonus system and want a collective, tax-advantaged vehicle to channel it to all staff.
- **Example:** The mechanism is defined by Finland's labour authority (YT-asiamies / the Co-operation Ombudsman): 'A personnel fund is a fund owned and controlled by the personnel, with the purpose of managing the performance bonus or profit bonus items.'  
  Source: https://yt-asiamies.fi/en/general-information-on-personnel-funds
- **Notes:** Definition, ownership/control by personnel, Act 934/2010, voluntary nature and minimum-staff/income thresholds verified from the official Finnish YT-asiamies pages. Distinct from equity ownership - it is a collective profit-sharing fund. (Different sources cite 5 vs 10 minimum employees; YT-asiamies states at least 5 plus an income minimum.)


## employee-owned company (distinct from cooperative)

### Sociedad Laboral (SAL / SLL) - worker-owned company  ·  ⚑ likely unfamiliar to a generalist VC

- **What it is:** A Spanish commercial company (a public limited or limited-liability company) in which the majority of the share capital is owned by employees who have permanent, full-time employment contracts - legally distinct from a worker cooperative.
- **How it works:** It is incorporated as a normal Sociedad Anonima or Sociedad de Responsabilidad Limitada but carries the 'Laboral' label (S.A.L. or S.L.L.). Most of the capital must belong to worker-members with open-ended contracts, and no single partner may hold more than 33.33% of the capital (up to 50% if the partner is a public institution or non-profit). Permanent workers have preferential rights to acquire shares. It must register with the Registro de Sociedades Laborales.
- **Legal regime / geography:** Spain. Governed by Law 44/2015 of 14 October on Worker-Owned and Participated Companies (which repealed Law 4/1997); Royal Decree 2114/1998 also applies. First legislated in 1986.
- **Who uses it:** Spanish SMEs, often created when employees take over a firm; distinct from cooperatives in that it uses standard company law with a capital-ownership rule rather than cooperative governance.
- **Example:** As of Q4 2018, Spain had 9,234 sociedades laborales generating 62,175 jobs (CEPES, the Spanish Business Confederation of the Social Economy).  
  Source: https://www.cepes.es/social/entidades_sociedades_laborables&lng=en
- **Notes:** Verified from CEPES: max 33.33% per partner (50% for public/non-profit), governed by Law 44/2015 and RD 2114/1998, 9,234 companies / 62,175 jobs (Q4 2018). Key distinction from Mondragon-style co-ops: this is a capital-share company with a worker-majority rule, not a one-member-one-vote cooperative - do not merge the two.


## statutory profit-sharing

### Participation (mandatory profit-sharing, France)  ·  ⚑ likely unfamiliar to a generalist VC

- **What it is:** A French legal mandate requiring companies above a size threshold to redistribute a portion of their profits to all employees via a formula fixed by law, building employee savings rather than tying payouts to individual performance.
- **How it works:** Companies compute a 'special participation reserve' using a statutory formula tied to company profit; the resulting sum is shared among all employees (no individual-performance criteria in the legal formula) and typically placed into employee-savings vehicles, often locked for a period with tax advantages. It associates the whole workforce with company results collectively.
- **Legal regime / geography:** France. Created by the ordonnance of 17 August 1967 under de Gaulle ('participation des salaries aux fruits de l'expansion des entreprises'); mandatory for firms over 100 employees originally, lowered to 50+ employees since 1990. From 1 January 2025, firms with 11-49 employees must also adopt a value-sharing device (participation, interessement, a value-sharing bonus, or savings-plan top-up).
- **Who uses it:** All French enterprises of at least 50 employees that make a profit (mandatory); the concept is collective profit distribution, not equity ownership.
- **Example:** The regime itself is the example: per the French Ministry of the Economy, participation 'permet de redistribuer a vos salaries une partie des benefices realises par votre entreprise' and is obligatory in companies of at least 50 employees.  
  Source: https://www.economie.gouv.fr/interessement-participation
- **Notes:** Obligatory-at-50-employees and the law-fixed formula verified via service-public/code.travail.gouv.fr and economie.gouv.fr; 1967 ordonnance (originally 100 employees, 50 since 1990) verified via clesdusocial/eres. Distinct from interessement - keep as separate rows. economie.gouv.fr returned 403 on direct fetch but the definition is corroborated across official French sources.


## statutory profit-sharing (voluntary scheme)

### Interessement (voluntary profit/performance-sharing, France)  ·  ⚑ likely unfamiliar to a generalist VC

- **What it is:** A French optional value-sharing scheme letting any company tie employee bonuses to the company's results or performance, under a collective agreement, with tax/social advantages - separate from and additional to mandatory participation.
- **How it works:** An employer sets up an interessement agreement defining how bonuses are calculated against the firm's results or performance targets; unlike participation, the formula is set by the company's collective agreement and may use individual criteria (e.g. time present, salary level), and it is not based purely on profit. It is facultatif (optional) and carries social-charge/tax exemptions when conditions are met.
- **Legal regime / geography:** France. Part of the French 'epargne salariale' (employee savings) framework in the Code du travail; complements the mandatory participation regime.
- **Who uses it:** French companies of any size that voluntarily want to share upside and motivate staff; especially used by firms below the participation threshold or to add to it.
- **Example:** The regime itself is the example: per the French Ministry of the Economy, interessement is 'un dispositif facultatif qui vous permet d'interesser vos salaries aux resultats ou performances de votre entreprise'.  
  Source: https://www.economie.gouv.fr/interessement-participation
- **Notes:** Voluntary status and the results/performance (not pure profit) basis verified via service-public.fr and economie.gouv.fr. Deliberately kept as a separate row from participation because the two regimes differ (mandatory vs voluntary; statutory profit formula vs company-defined criteria).


## steward-ownership

### Doppelstiftung (double-foundation model)  ·  ⚑ likely unfamiliar to a generalist VC

- **What it is:** A German ownership structure that splits a company's economic ownership from its voting control: a charitable foundation holds the large majority of the share capital (dividends fund charity), while a separate trust/entity holds the majority of the voting rights to steer the company long-term.
- **How it works:** Almost all share capital is transferred to a charitable foundation (Stiftung), which receives dividends to fund its philanthropic purpose but is largely kept out of operational governance. The voting rights attached to those shares are transferred to a separate industrial-trust entity (often itself a foundation or KG) controlled by experienced managers/family representatives. This separation lets the firm stay unlisted and plan over decades free from stock-market pressure.
- **Legal regime / geography:** Germany. Built from foundation law (Stiftungsrecht) and company/partnership law; a private structuring choice, not a single statutory form.
- **Who uses it:** Large German family enterprises wanting permanent independence plus a charitable legacy (e.g. Bosch, Zeiss, Bertelsmann-type structures).
- **Example:** Robert Bosch GmbH: the Robert Bosch Stiftung holds around 94% of the share capital, while the voting rights are held by Robert Bosch Industrietreuhand KG; the founding family holds the remainder.  
  Source: https://www.bosch-stiftung.de/en/about-robert-bosch-stiftung
- **Notes:** Verified from Robert Bosch Stiftung's own site: '~94 percent of the shares', voting rights 'transferred to Robert Bosch Industrietreuhand KG'; foundation established 1964. This is the canonical double-foundation case and a sub-type of steward-ownership.

### Verantwortungseigentum (steward-ownership)  ·  ⚑ likely unfamiliar to a generalist VC

- **What it is:** A German/European ownership philosophy ('ownership of responsibility') in which a company has no profit-extracting external shareholders: control is held by stewards inside the business who have no personal claim on profits, and the company effectively owns itself in service of its purpose.
- **How it works:** Built on two principles: (1) self-governance - voting control stays with people active in the business and cannot be bought by outside capital; (2) an asset-lock - profits and capital cannot be extracted for private gain but are reinvested, used to repay capped investor returns, shared, or donated. Because Germany has no dedicated legal form, it is implemented today via existing company + foundation law using one of three constructions: the veto-share model, the single-foundation model, or the double-foundation model.
- **Legal regime / geography:** Germany (and similar in Netherlands/Austria/Switzerland). Currently NOT an enacted standalone legal form - implemented through existing GmbH + Stiftung law. A draft bill for a new 'GmbH with locked capital' (GmbH mit gebundenem Vermoegen / GmbH gebV), formerly nicknamed VE-GmbH, was developed by an academic working group (2024) at the request of parliamentarians but has not been enacted.
- **Who uses it:** Founders and family firms wanting to make purpose and independence permanent; mission-driven and impact companies. The Stiftung Verantwortungseigentum (founded November 2019) connects steward-owned companies in Germany and lobbies for a legal framework.
- **Example:** Ecosia (the search engine) transitioned to steward-ownership in 2018 with the Purpose Foundation, adopting a model where shares cannot be sold at a profit, no profits can be extracted, and the company can never be sold.  
  Source: https://blog.ecosia.org/trees-not-profits/
- **Notes:** Status verified via Luther/Gleiss Lutz/ADVANT Beiten analyses and Stiftung Verantwortungseigentum: the dedicated legal form is a DRAFT BILL, not enacted - a common error is to state it 'is a legal form'. Ecosia structure verified from Ecosia's own blog and Purpose Economy.


## steward-ownership / ownership-control separation

### Stichting Administratiekantoor (STAK) with depositary receipts  ·  ⚑ likely unfamiliar to a generalist VC

- **What it is:** A Dutch trust-office foundation that holds the legal title and voting rights to a company's shares and issues 'depositary receipts' (certificaten) to beneficiaries, splitting voting control (kept by the foundation's board/stewards) from the economic benefit (dividends, flowing to receipt-holders).
- **How it works:** Shares are transferred ('certified', certificering) to the STAK, which becomes the legal shareholder and exercises the votes via its board. In exchange it issues depositary receipts to the former owners or successors, who receive the economic value (dividends) but not the votes. A STAK has no shareholders or share capital - in effect it owns itself - so it can serve as a durable steward/control vehicle (e.g. a founder/board keeps control while economic ownership passes to family or stakeholders).
- **Legal regime / geography:** Netherlands. Dutch foundation (stichting) law; established by deed before a civil-law notary with Trust Conditions (Administratievoorwaarden). A long-standing, widely used Dutch structure.
- **Who uses it:** Dutch family businesses, founders, and listed companies wanting control protection or succession with separated economics; also used for steward-ownership and anti-takeover protection.
- **Example:** Heineken and Anheuser-Busch InBev are cited as well-known companies that use STAK-type structures to keep closely connected family/founder control while economics are held via certificates.  
  Source: https://www.ocorian.com/knowledge-hub/insights/understanding-dutch-stak-strategic-tool-ownership-and-governance
- **Notes:** STAK mechanics verified from Ocorian and other Dutch advisory sources (the foundation holds legal title + votes; receipt-holders keep dividends; 'a STAK owns itself'). The Heineken/AB InBev examples are from advisory-source reporting (snippet-level), so they are attributed here as cited examples rather than independently primary-verified company filings.


## steward-ownership intermediary (enabler, not a legal form)

### Purpose Foundation / golden-share steward-ownership enabler  ·  ⚑ likely unfamiliar to a generalist VC

- **What it is:** A non-profit intermediary that helps companies lock in steward-ownership by taking a tiny 'golden' veto share, ensuring the company's purpose and the no-sale/no-profit-extraction principles can never be reversed by future owners.
- **How it works:** The foundation typically takes ~1% of voting rights plus a veto right over any change to the company's articles that would contradict steward-ownership principles, and capped dividend rights. It does not run the company; it acts as a permanent external guardian of the asset-lock and self-governance commitments. It is an enabler/advisor and guardian, not itself a statutory ownership regime.
- **Legal regime / geography:** Germany / international (Purpose Foundation operates across Europe and beyond). It is an organization/intermediary that uses existing company and foundation law; it is not a legal entity type like an EOT or SCOP.
- **Who uses it:** Founders converting their companies to steward-ownership who want an independent third party to hold the veto/golden share; mission-driven firms.
- **Example:** Ecosia's 'golden share' is held by the Purpose Foundation, which has ~1% of voting rights, a veto against changes to the articles that contradict steward-ownership, and capped dividend rights.  
  Source: https://purpose-economy.org/en/companies/ecosia/
- **Notes:** Important: category is intermediary/enabler, NOT a parallel statutory regime to EOT/SCOP. Verified via Purpose Economy and Ecosia sources. The veto/golden-share mechanism is the operative tool.


## trust-based employee ownership

### Indirect employee ownership via employee benefit trust (John Lewis Partnership model)

- **What it is:** A long-established UK form of 100% indirect employee ownership in which the company is held in trust permanently for the benefit of its employees ('Partners'), who share in profits and governance but do not personally own or trade shares.
- **How it works:** The founder placed the business into an irrevocable settlement in trust so it would be held for employees 'present and prospective'. Partners receive an annual share of profits (the Partnership Bonus, a single percentage of pay for everyone) and exercise voice through democratic bodies (Partnership Council, Partnership Board, Chairman) under a written Constitution. This predates and is structurally distinct from the 2014 statutory EOT.
- **Legal regime / geography:** United Kingdom. Trust/company law; the original 1929 irrevocable trust settlement plus the John Lewis Partnership written Constitution. Not created under the Finance Act 2014 EOT regime.
- **Who uses it:** Large mature businesses choosing permanent collective employee ownership; the John Lewis Partnership is the UK's largest employee-owned business. The trust-ownership idea inspired the later statutory EOT.
- **Example:** John Lewis Partnership (parent of John Lewis and Waitrose) - 'the UK's largest employee owned business', owned in trust for its Partners, with c. 65,000 Partners.  
  Source: https://www.johnlewispartnership.co.uk/about.html
- **Notes:** Verified directly from John Lewis Partnership's own site (https://www.johnlewispartnership.co.uk/about.html): 'the UK's largest employee owned business', 'c. 65,000 Partners'. Founding-in-trust (1929 irrevocable settlement, business given to workers 'present and prospective') from eoa and parliamentary sources (those specific pages 403/404'd on fetch, so the 1929 date is reported, not primary-verified here). ## worker buyout (statutory financing framework)

### Marcora Law worker buyout (workers' buyout / WBO)  ·  ⚑ likely unfamiliar to a generalist VC

- **What it is:** An Italian legal framework that lets employees of a company facing closure pool their would-be unemployment benefits as a lump sum to capitalize a new worker cooperative that buys out the failing firm, backed by matching public/cooperative finance.
- **How it works:** Redundant workers can take an advance lump sum of up to roughly three years of unemployment benefits and invest it as share capital in a new worker cooperative buying the business. Dedicated institutional investors (notably CFI - Cooperazione/Compagnia Finanziaria Industriale - and historically Foncooper) add matching capital alongside the workers' contribution. Because workers trade periodic benefits for an equivalent lump sum, the program is designed to be broadly cost-neutral to the state while funding the buyout.
- **Legal regime / geography:** Italy. Established by the Marcora Law (Law no. 49/1985, passed 27 February 1985), named after minister Giovanni Marcora; restructured after 2000 (e.g. matching shifted toward a 1:1 ratio).
- **Who uses it:** Employees of insolvent or closing Italian firms (often industrial SMEs) who recuperate the business as a cooperative; financed through CFI and regional cooperative funds.
- **Example:** CFI (Cooperazione Finanza Impresa), the public-cooperative financing institution under the Marcora framework, reports having made investments totaling around EUR 170m that helped create more than 13,000 jobs and intervene in over 300 companies.  
  Source: https://ica.coop/en/media/news/marcora-law-supporting-worker-buyouts-thirty-years
- **Notes:** Law 49/1985, 27 Feb 1985 and the lump-sum-of-unemployment-benefits mechanism verified via ICA and Wikipreneurship; CFI/Foncooper roles and ~EUR 170m / 13,000 jobs / 300+ companies figures from ICA reporting. No single named buyout firm is given as the example - CFI-as-institution plus its aggregate stats is the verified real example.


## worker cooperative

### Societe cooperative et participative (SCOP)  ·  ⚑ likely unfamiliar to a generalist VC

- **What it is:** A French worker/production cooperative in which employees are the majority owner-members, holding at least the majority of capital and the majority of voting rights on a one-person-one-vote basis, with profits shared substantially with the workforce.
- **How it works:** Employees hold the majority of the share capital and at least 65% of voting rights; governance is one-member-one-vote regardless of capital invested. A significant share of profits is allocated to employees and to indivisible reserves that build the cooperative's long-term capital. Managers are elected by the worker-members. SCOPs can be formed as new ventures, healthy-business transmissions, association conversions, or rescues of failing firms.
- **Legal regime / geography:** France. Governed by cooperative law (notably the law of 19 July 1978 on SCOPs and the broader 1947 cooperation law / 2014 Social and Solidarity Economy law). 'SCOP' = societe cooperative de production / et participative.
- **Who uses it:** French SMEs across services, construction, industry and culture; commonly used for employee-led business succession. In 2024 France had 2,723 SCOPs employing more than 62,685 people.
- **Example:** Upcoop (formerly Groupe Cheque Dejeuner), a SCOP founded in 1964 that designs payment/benefit solutions, is 100% owned by its employees with no external shareholders and had about 3,200 employees in 2024.  
  Source: https://www.les-scop.coop/chiffres-cles-2025
- **Notes:** Sector statistics (2,723 SCOPs, 62,685+ employees in 2024) from the official confederation les-scop.coop. Upcoop/Cheque Dejeuner example (1964, 100% employee-owned, ~3,200 employees) from Upcoop and les-scop-idf sources. The 65%-of-votes / majority-of-capital rule is the standard SCOP requirement.

### Worker cooperative (Mondragon model)

- **What it is:** A network of industrial, retail, finance and knowledge cooperatives in the Spanish Basque Country owned and democratically governed by their worker-members on a one-member-one-vote basis, with deliberately compressed pay ratios.
- **How it works:** Each cooperative is owned by its working members, who elect governing bodies on a one-person-one-vote basis (not by capital share) and vote on strategy and pay solidarity at general assemblies. Wage ratios between the highest- and lowest-paid are capped (commonly cited around 6:1, set democratically per co-op). The cooperatives are federated, sharing finance (its own cooperative bank historically, Caja Laboral), training and inter-cooperative solidarity funds.
- **Legal regime / geography:** Spain (Basque Country). Spanish and Basque cooperative law; Mondragon is a corporation/federation of independent cooperatives, not a single company.
- **Who uses it:** Industrial manufacturers, the Eroski retail chain, finance and education entities within the Mondragon group; a global reference model for large-scale worker cooperativism.
- **Example:** MONDRAGON Corporation: describes itself as a 'cooperative socioeconomic project', comprising 81 self-governing cooperatives with approximately 70,000 people and a 'one person, one vote system'.  
  Source: https://www.mondragon-corporation.com/en/about-us/
- **Notes:** 81 cooperatives, ~70,000 people, one-person-one-vote verified from Mondragon's own about-us page. The 6:1 pay-ratio cap is widely reported (CSMonitor / ACI) but Mondragon's own page references 'wage solidarity' without a single number, and ratios actually vary by co-op (~3:1 to 9:1). Mondragon is a famous case a well-read VC may know - hence likely_unknown_to_ethan = false.
